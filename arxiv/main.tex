\author{Samuil Petkov}

\begin{abstract}
The dual degree of a vertex is the average degree of its neighbours. WOW-284 conjectures that the minimum dual degree is at most minus the least eigenvalue of the distance matrix. The graph is required to be finite, simple and connected, with at least three vertices and girth at least five. We give exact counterexamples on \(38,39,40,42\), and \(50\) vertices, for which the inequality is strictly reversed.

For regular graphs of girth at least five and diameter three, a polynomial identity relates the distance and adjacency matrices. It turns strict failure of WOW-284 into an interval condition on adjacency eigenvalues. This condition also bounds the order without a diameter assumption. Let \(G\) be finite, simple, connected and \(k\)-regular, with girth at least five and integer \(k\ge4\). If every nonprincipal adjacency eigenvalue \(\theta\) satisfies \(|\theta+1|\le\sqrt{2k-2}\), then \(|V(G)|<(k+2)(k^2+3)/6\). For regular counterexamples of diameter three, an integer matrix derived from the optimal polynomial gives stronger restrictions.

Deleting any set of at most five vertices from the Hoffman--Singleton graph leaves a strict counterexample. We give a six-vertex deletion for which strictness fails. If a degree-57 Moore graph of diameter two exists, deleting any set of at most \(56\) vertices leaves a connected strict counterexample. The finite computations use exact arithmetic.
\end{abstract}

\section{WOW-284 and the main results}\label{sec:introduction}

Let \(G\) be a finite simple connected graph of order
\(n=|V(G)|\ge2\).  Let \(A=A(G)\) and \(D=D(G)\) be its adjacency and
distance matrices, let \(I\) and \(J\) denote the identity and all-ones
matrices of order \(n\), and let \(\one\) be the all-ones vector.  Write
\(d(v)\) for the degree of \(v\), \(N(v)\) for its open neighbourhood, and, for
\(S\subseteq V(G)\), let \(G-S\) denote the subgraph induced by
\(V(G)\setminus S\).  Define
\[
 d^*(v)=\frac1{d(v)}\sum_{u\in N(v)}d(u),
 \qquad
 \delta^*(G)=\min_{v\in V(G)}d^*(v).
\]
Here \(d_G(u,v)\) is the length of a shortest path from \(u\) to \(v\),
so \(D=(d_G(u,v))_{u,v\in V(G)}\). Its eigenvalues are ordered as
\[
 \partial_1(G)\ge\cdots\ge\partial_n(G),
 \qquad \partial_n(G)=\lambda_{\min}(D(G)).
\]
Aouchiche and Hansen record the following Graffiti conjecture as
Conjecture~7.16 and attribute it to Fajtlowicz's 1998
\emph{Written on the Wall} report
\cite{Fajtlowicz1998} (see also \cite[Conjecture~7.16]{AouchicheHansen2014}).

\begin{wowconjecture}
If \(G\) has order at least three and girth at least five, then
\[
 \delta^*(G)\le-\lambda_{\min}(D(G)).
\]
\end{wowconjecture}

For every finite simple connected graph of order at least two, put
\[
 \Phi(G)=\delta^*(G)+\lambda_{\min}(D(G)).
\]
Among graphs satisfying the conjecture's hypotheses, \(G\) is a strict
counterexample precisely when \(\Phi(G)>0\).

The initial disproof is short.  A Moore graph of diameter two is a regular
graph whose radius-two ball contains every vertex and attains the order bound imposed by its degree.
A degree-\(k\) Moore graph of diameter two
\cite{HoffmanSingleton1960} has
\[
 A^2=(k-1)I-A+J,
 \qquad
 D=2J-2I-A,
\]
and hence
\[
 \delta^*(G)=k,
 \qquad
 \lambda_{\min}(D)=-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2.
\]
The conjecture holds on these graphs exactly for \(k\le3\), with equality at
\(k=3\), and fails for every realizable \(k>3\).  The degree-seven
Hoffman--Singleton graph therefore gives a gap of three.

The distance-polynomial viewpoint is established for minimal cages and
distance-polynomial graphs
\cite{HowladerPanigrahi2022,Fiol2016}.  Nonbacktracking linear-programming
bounds are due to Nozaki \cite{Nozaki2015}; related spectral-Moore work appears
in \cite{CioabaEtAl2016}.  We specialize these methods to the interval imposed by WOW-284 and to
vertex deletion. Our examples are not claimed to exhaust all
counterexamples, and the order-$38$ example is not claimed minimal.
Appendix~\ref{sec:verification} records the exact computations and the
scope of the Lean verification.

For a connected regular graph, we call the adjacency eigenvalues other than
its degree eigenvalue nonprincipal.

The paper follows two lines of argument. For regular graphs of diameter
three, the distance-polynomial identity turns strict failure of WOW-284 into
an interval condition on the nonprincipal adjacency eigenvalues
(Theorem~\ref{thm:diameter-three-score}). A polynomial trace bound restricts
the order. Evaluating the optimal polynomial at the adjacency matrix also
gives a matrix of integer combinations of short-walk counts. Subtracting a
constant all-ones term and adjusting the diagonal gives the integral excess matrix of
Theorem~\ref{thm:integral-slack}. The excess matrix has integral entries, while the associated slack matrix
has nonnegative principal minors. Together these give stronger order and cycle constraints. These lead to the three-to-one bound
(Theorem~\ref{thm:three-to-one}) and necessary conditions at the unresolved
degree-six, order-\(50\) boundary. The exact polynomial optimum is proved in
Section~\ref{sec:lp}. It is an optimum within the stated polynomial class,
not a claim that the graph-order bound is best possible.

The degree and diameter restrictions narrow the search: every regular strict
counterexample has degree at least six and diameter at most four, and a
diameter-four example would have degree at least ten
(Theorems~\ref{thm:regular-degree-six}, \ref{thm:endpoint-diameter},
and~\ref{thm:diameter-four}). We give the detailed arithmetic and cycle
restrictions where they enter the proofs.

The second argument concerns deletion. Deleting vertices can increase the
distances between those left behind, so taking a principal submatrix of the
old distance matrix is not enough. For small deletions from a Moore graph,
we identify exactly which distances increase. This correction gives general
stability bounds (Section~\ref{sec:punctures});
Appendix~\ref{app:small-deletion-spectra} gives the exact one- and
two-deletion spectra. For Hoffman--Singleton, the bounds prove
strictness through three deletions; exact orbit certificates handle four
and five. A six-vertex deletion shows that the universal radius is five
(Theorem~\ref{thm:hs-radius}).

\section{Dual degree, Moore graphs, and explicit counterexamples}
\label{sec:examples}

\subsection{Dual degree and the size of a radius-two ball}

Dual degree has a simple local interpretation when triangles and
four-cycles are absent: it measures the size of a radius-two ball relative
to the degree of its centre.  This count will also explain why diameter-two
examples are Moore graphs.

For \(v\in V(G)\), write \(\Gamma_i(v)\) for the distance-\(i\) sphere and
\(B_2(v)=\Gamma_0(v)\cup\Gamma_1(v)\cup\Gamma_2(v)\).

\begin{proposition}[Second-degree identity]\label{prop:radius-two}
If \(G\) contains no triangle and no \(4\)-cycle, then
\[
 |B_2(v)|=1+\sum_{u\in N(v)}d(u),
 \qquad
 d^*(v)=\frac{|B_2(v)|-1}{d(v)}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For distinct neighbours \(u,w\) of \(v\), the sets
\(N(u)\setminus\{v\}\) and \(N(w)\setminus\{v\}\) are disjoint; an
intersection would form a \(4\)-cycle, and a member in \(N(v)\) would form a
triangle.  These sets partition \(\Gamma_2(v)\), so
\[
 |\Gamma_2(v)|=\sum_{u\in N(v)}(d(u)-1).
\]
Adding the centre and first sphere proves the first identity, which is
Backelin's Lemma~2.1 \cite{Backelin2015}; division by \(d(v)\) gives the
normalized second identity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{When Moore graphs violate WOW-284}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:moore-threshold}
Let \(M\) be a degree-\(k\) Moore graph of diameter two, \(k\ge2\).  Then
\[
 |V(M)|=k^2+1,
 \qquad g(M)=5,
 \qquad \delta^*(M)=k,
\]
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(D(M))=-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2,
\]
and
\[
 \Phi(M)=k-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2.
\]
Thus \(M\) satisfies WOW-284 exactly for \(k\le3\), with equality exactly at
\(k=3\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Attainment of the Moore bound determines every pair of vertices: adjacent
vertices have no common neighbour, and nonadjacent vertices have exactly
one.  It also gives a five-cycle through every edge.  Indeed, given an edge
\(uv\), choose \(x\in N(u)\setminus\{v\}\) and
\(y\in N(v)\setminus\{u\}\).  The vertices \(x,y\) cannot be adjacent,
since that would create a four-cycle; their unique common neighbour
therefore completes a five-cycle through \(uv\).  The common-neighbour
counts give the matrix identity
\[
 A^2=(k-1)I-A+J.
\]
On \(\one^\perp\), the nonprincipal adjacency eigenvalues are the roots
\[
 r,s=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{4k-3}}2.
\]
If \(m_r,m_s\) are their multiplicities, then
\[
 m_r+m_s=k^2,\qquad k+m_rr+m_ss=0.
\]
Consequently
\[
 m_r=\frac{k(k\sqrt{4k-3}+k-2)}{2\sqrt{4k-3}},
 \qquad
 m_s=\frac{k(k\sqrt{4k-3}-k+2)}{2\sqrt{4k-3}},
\]
and both roots occur.  We can now pass from adjacency to distance
eigenvalues.  Every nonedge has distance two, so \(D=2J-2I-A\), and the
least distance eigenvalue is \(-2-r=-(3+\sqrt{4k-3})/2\).
Since regularity gives \(\delta^*=k\), comparison with the dual degree
reduces to
\[
 (2k-3)^2-(4k-3)=4(k-1)(k-3)
\]
This gives the stated threshold.  The exact scalar and finite checks are independently
repeated by \codefile{scripts/verify_regular_score_calculus.py}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Hoffman--Singleton graph in coordinates}

All subscripts below lie in \(\F=\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z\).  Let
\[
 V(M)=\{P_{i,j}:i,j\in\F\}\mathbin{\dot\cup}
      \{Q_{k,\ell}:k,\ell\in\F\},
\]
with edges
\begin{align*}
 P_{i,j}&\sim P_{i,j\pm1},\\
 Q_{k,\ell}&\sim Q_{k,\ell\pm2},\\
 P_{i,j}&\sim Q_{k,ik+j}.
\end{align*}
This is Hafner's affine-coordinate form of the Hoffman--Singleton graph after a
minor reindexing \cite{Hafner2003}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:hs-coordinate}
The coordinate construction is a simple connected \(7\)-regular graph on
\(50\) vertices.  Adjacent pairs have no common neighbour and nonadjacent pairs
have exactly one.  Consequently it has girth five, diameter two, and
\[
 \Spec D(M)=\{91^{(1)},1^{(21)},(-4)^{(28)}\}.
\]
In particular, \(\delta^*(M)=7\) and \(\Phi(M)=3\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The neighbourhoods are
\begin{align*}
N(P_{i,j})&=\{P_{i,j-1},P_{i,j+1}\}
 \cup\{Q_{k,ik+j}:k\in\F\},\\
N(Q_{k,\ell})&=\{Q_{k,\ell-2},Q_{k,\ell+2}\}
 \cup\{P_{i,\ell-ik}:i\in\F\}.
\end{align*}
Each neighbourhood has seven distinct entries.  To check the
common-neighbour rule, first take two \(P\)-vertices in the same layer.
The \(5\)-cycle contributes no common neighbour for an adjacent pair and
one for a nonadjacent pair; a common \(Q\)-neighbour would force their
second coordinates to agree.  For vertices in distinct \(P\)-layers, the
equation \((i-i')k=j'-j\) determines a unique common \(Q\)-neighbour.
The \(Q\)-vertices satisfy the same rule: within a layer the
\(5\)-cycle uses steps \(\pm2\), and between layers there is a unique
common \(P\)-neighbour.  Finally, for a cross pair
\(P_{i,j},Q_{k,\ell}\), put \(r=\ell-(ik+j)\).  The pair is adjacent
when \(r=0\); it has one common \(P\)-neighbour when
\(r\in\{\pm1\}\), and one common \(Q\)-neighbour when
\(r\in\{\pm2\}\).  These cases exhaust the five residues.  The claimed geometry
now follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:moore-threshold}.  For degree \(7\), the
multiplicity equations in its proof give adjacency multiplicities \(28\) at
\(2\) and \(21\) at \(-3\), and hence the displayed distance spectrum.  The exhaustive
pair certificate, integer distances from breadth-first search (BFS), characteristic polynomial, and exact
positive-definiteness check are in \codefile{scripts/verify_exact.py}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Smaller counterexamples and second distance spheres}

The same coordinates give smaller counterexamples by deleting a Petersen
subgraph and then one or two further vertices.  A second subconstituent gives
another regular example.

Let
\[
 \mathcal P=\{P_{0,j},Q_{0,j}:j\in\F\}.
\]
The induced graph \(M[\mathcal P]\) is a Petersen graph.  Put
\[
 R=M-\mathcal P,
 \quad H_{39}=R-P_{1,0},
 \quad H_{38}=R-\{P_{1,0},P_{1,1}\},
\]
and let \(X_{42}\) be the second subconstituent of \(P_{0,0}\), namely the
graph induced by the vertices at distance two from it.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:explicit-examples}
The following are strict counterexamples.
\[
\begin{array}{c@{\quad}c@{\quad}c@{\quad}c}
\toprule
G&|V(G)|&\delta^*(G)&\lambda_{\min}(D(G))\\
\midrule
H_{38}&38&17/3&-3-\sqrt7\\
H_{39}&39&35/6&>-35/6\\
R&40&6&-5\\
X_{42}&42&6&-5\\
M&50&7&-4\\
\bottomrule
\end{array}
\]
The entry for \(H_{39}\) records an exact strict lower bound obtained from
positive definiteness of \(6D+35I\); it is not a decimal approximation to the
least eigenvalue.  Moreover, all \(40\) labelled singleton deletions of \(R\)
and all \(120\) labelled deletions of the endpoints of an edge of \(R\) are
strict counterexamples; within each family, the distance characteristic
polynomial is constant.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The regular examples can be read from known adjacency spectra.  The graph
\(R\) is the Anstee--Robertson graph, the unique \((6,5)\)-cage.
O'Keefe and Wong proved minimality, and Wong proved uniqueness
\cite{OKeefeWong1979,Wong1979}; its realization as a Petersen deletion of the
Hoffman--Singleton graph also appears in
\cite[pp.~262--263]{KlinMuzychukZivAv2009}.
Here is a direct derivation of its spectrum. Write \(B\) for the
adjacency matrix of the deleted Petersen graph, \(A_R=A(R)\), and \(C\)
for the \(10\times40\) incidence matrix between \(\mathcal P\) and \(R\).
A vertex of \(R\) has at most one neighbour in \(\mathcal P\): two adjacent
neighbours would make a triangle, while two nonadjacent Petersen vertices
already have their unique common neighbour in \(\mathcal P\).
There are \(10(7-3)=40\) edges between the two parts. Thus each vertex of
\(R\) has exactly one neighbour in \(\mathcal P\), and \(R\) is \(6\)-regular.

The Petersen identity \(B^2=2I-B+J\) and the blocks of the Moore identity give
\[
 CC^{\mathsf T}=4I,\qquad
 A_RC^{\mathsf T}=J_{40,10}-C^{\mathsf T}-C^{\mathsf T}B.
\]
The image of \(C^{\mathsf T}\) therefore has dimension ten. The Petersen
eigenvalues \(1^{(5)}\) and \((-2)^{(4)}\) give eigenvalues
\((-2)^{(5)}\) and \(1^{(4)}\) on this image; the constant vector gives
the eigenvalue \(6\). Its orthogonal complement is \(\ker C\).
On this \(30\)-dimensional space, the other diagonal block gives
\[
 A_R^2+A_R-6I=0.
\]
Hence its eigenvalues are \(2\) and \(-3\). The dimension and trace
equations give multiplicities \(18\) and \(12\), respectively. We obtain
\[
 \Spec A_R=\{6^{(1)},2^{(18)},1^{(4)},(-2)^{(5)},(-3)^{(12)}\}.
\]

It remains to check the diameter before applying the distance polynomial.
Let \(x,y\in V(R)\) be nonadjacent. If their common neighbour in \(M\)
survives, they remain at distance two. Otherwise they share a deleted
Petersen neighbour. Choose \(a\in N_R(x)\). Girth five makes \(a,y\)
nonadjacent, and makes the Petersen neighbour of \(a\) different from that
of \(y\). Their unique common neighbour therefore lies in \(R\), giving a
path of length three from \(x\) to \(y\).
Each deleted vertex has four neighbours in \(R\), so pairs whose common
neighbour was deleted do occur. Thus \(R\) is connected with diameter three.
Theorem~\ref{thm:diameter-three-score} now gives
\[
 \Spec D(R)=\{75^{(1)},3^{(5)},0^{(16)},(-5)^{(18)}\},
 \qquad \Phi(R)=1.
\]
For the other regular example, the second-subconstituent calculation gives
\[
 \Spec D(X_{42})=\{81^{(1)},4^{(6)},0^{(14)},(-5)^{(21)}\}.
\]
The classical second-subconstituent identification and adjacency spectrum are
recorded in \cite[Table~3, p.~265]{vanDamHaemers2003}.
The two irregular examples require separate certificates.  For
\(H_{38}\), a direct degree count gives \(\delta^*=17/3\).
The factor \(x^2+6x+2\) supplies the root \(-3-\sqrt7\), and exact
Sturm isolation certifies that it is the least root.  For \(H_{39}\),
strictness is certified directly: the exact matrix \(6D+35I\) is
positive definite.  All graph, girth, distance, dual-degree,
Sturm, and rational \(LDL^{\mathsf T}\) certificates are checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_extended.py}.  The characteristic polynomials for the two labelled deletion families are
recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:small-example-certificates}.
The labelled deletion families are exhausted by
\codefile{scripts/verify_descendant_families.py}.  No transitivity or
numerical root ordering is used.  Fixed graph6, adjacency-list, and edge-list
records are provided in \path{data/graphs/}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Distance-two spheres in Moore graphs}

\begin{theorem}[Moore second subconstituents]\label{thm:second-subconstituent}
Let \(M\) be a degree-\(K\) Moore graph of diameter two, \(K\ge3\), and let
\(X\) be the graph induced by \(\Gamma_2(v)\) for a fixed vertex \(v\).  Then
\(X\) has order \(K(K-1)\), degree \(K-1\), girth at least five, diameter
three, and
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(D(X))=-\frac{5+\sqrt{4K-3}}2.
\]
Consequently
\[
 \Phi(X)=K-1-\frac{5+\sqrt{4K-3}}2,
\]
which is positive exactly for integers \(K\ge6\).
\end{theorem}

Each vertex in the second sphere loses its unique neighbour in the
first sphere.  This gives the order, degree, and girth assertions directly.
The remaining calculation splits the adjacency operator according to their
neighbours in the first sphere, then checks that the induced graph has diameter
three.  Appendix~\ref{app:second-sphere-proof} gives the complete proof and
the multiplicity calculation.

\section{Distance spectra from adjacency eigenvalues}\label{sec:score-calculus}

Let \(A_i\) have entry one on pairs of vertices at distance \(i\), and
zero elsewhere. For a regular graph of diameter three, the girth assumption
identifies the distance-two matrix with a quadratic in the adjacency matrix.  The
distance-three matrix is then the remaining part of the all-ones matrix.
As a result, the nonprincipal distance eigenvalues can be read directly
from the adjacency eigenvalues.  This is the spectral reformulation of
WOW-284 that we use throughout the obstruction arguments.

\begin{theorem}[Diameter-three score formula]\label{thm:diameter-three-score}
Let \(G\) be connected, \(k\)-regular, of girth at least five and diameter
three, with adjacency matrix \(A\) and order \(n\).  Then
\[
 D=3J+(k-3)I-2A-A^2,
\]
\[
 D+kI=3J+(2k-2)I-(A+I)^2.
\]
The principal distance eigenvalue is \(3n-k^2-k-3\), and a nonprincipal
adjacency eigenvalue \(\theta\) gives the distance eigenvalue
\[
 \mu(\theta)=k-2-(\theta+1)^2.
\]
Consequently
\[
 \resultbox{
 \Phi(G)=2k-2-\max_{\theta\ne k}(\theta+1)^2.
 }
\]
Thus \(G\) is a strict counterexample exactly when every nonprincipal
adjacency eigenvalue \(\theta\) satisfies
\[
 |\theta+1|<\sqrt{2k-2}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We first count pairs at distance two.  Girth at least five means that
distinct vertices have at most one common neighbour, and adjacent vertices
have none.  The diagonal of the squared adjacency matrix counts the degree,
so \(A_2=A^2-kI\).  Because the diameter is three, the other distinct
nonadjacent pairs are at distance three; thus \(A_3=J-I-A-A_2\).
Putting these expressions into \(D=A+2A_2+3A_3\) proves both identities.

We next apply the identities to adjacency eigenvectors.  The matrix identities
and regularity make the constant vector an eigenvector of \(D\).  Its eigenvalue is positive
and is the Perron root, since every off-diagonal entry of the distance
matrix is positive.  On \(\one^\perp\), \(J\) acts as zero, so each
nonprincipal adjacency eigenvalue gives the stated distance eigenvalue.
Taking the least of these values and using \(\delta^*=k\) gives the score formula.
The spectral transfer and score calculations for the examples used below
are independently checked by \codefile{scripts/verify_regular_score_calculus.py}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Bipartite obstruction]\label{cor:bipartite}
A connected \(k\)-regular bipartite graph of girth at least five and diameter
three is not a strict counterexample for \(k\ge3\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The nonprincipal eigenvalue \(-k\) satisfies
\[
 (-k+1)^2-(2k-2)=(k-1)(k-3)\ge0
 \qquad(k\ge3).
\]
Thus the required strict inequality fails, with equality exactly at \(k=3\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Higher-diameter transfer]\label{prop:higher-transfer}
Let \(G\) be connected and \(k\)-regular, with diameter \(d\) and girth at
least \(2d-1\).  Let \(A_i\) denote the distance-\(i\) matrix, and define
\[
 F_0=1,
 \quad F_1=x,
 \quad F_2=x^2-k,
 \quad F_i=xF_{i-1}-(k-1)F_{i-2}\quad(i\ge3).
\]
Then \(A_i=F_i(A)\) for \(0\le i\le d-1\), and
\[
 D=dJ+q_d(A),
 \qquad
 q_d(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(i-d)F_i(x).
\]
In particular,
\[
 q_3(x)=k-3-2x-x^2,
\]
\[
 q_4(x)=-x^3-2x^2+(2k-4)x+2k-4.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The polynomial matrices count walks with no immediate reversal of an
edge at an internal step.  These are the nonbacktracking walks \cite[Theorem~1]{Nozaki2015}.
The girth condition makes this count agree with distance: up to length
\(d-1\), a nonbacktracking walk between two vertices is unique exactly
when its length is their graph distance.  Thus the distance-\(i\)
matrices are the corresponding polynomials in \(A\).
In the sum \(D=\sum_{i=0}^d iA_i\), the identity \(A_d=F_d(A)\) need not hold.
Eliminate \(A_d\) using \(J=\sum_{i=0}^d A_i\).
This proves the formula within the established distance-polynomial
framework \cite{HowladerPanigrahi2022,Fiol2016}.
\end{proof}

\section{Degree and diameter bounds for regular counterexamples}\label{sec:obstructions}

A long geodesic can force a distance eigenvalue below the threshold required
by WOW-284.  Small degree gives a different obstruction: the graph can have
too few vertices to fit the spectral window.  We begin with the geodesic
bound.  The low-degree argument uses the polynomial order bound proved in the next
section; its proof does not depend on the reductions made here.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:diam-rayleigh}
For every connected graph,
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(D(G))\le-\diam(G).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a diametral pair \(u,v\), the Rayleigh quotient of \(e_u-e_v\) is
\(-d_G(u,v)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:regular-degree-six}
Every connected regular strict counterexample to WOW-284 has degree at least
six.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let the degree of \(G\) be \(k\).  We may use the linear-programming (LP) bound in
Theorem~\ref{thm:lp-ceiling}, whose proof is independent of this degree
reduction.  Lemma~\ref{lem:diam-rayleigh} and strictness first give
\(\diam(G)<k\).  This already excludes \(k\le2\): the order satisfies
\(|V(G)|\ge3\), and the girth hypothesis excludes completeness, so
\(\diam(G)\ge2\ge k\).  For \(k=3\), the radius-two lower bound and
\(\diam(G)\le2\) force equality in the Moore bound.
Theorem~\ref{thm:moore-threshold} then gives \(\Phi(G)=0\), contradicting
the required \(\Phi(G)>0\).

For \(k=4\), the two possible diameters lead to different obstructions.
Diameter two would require a degree-four Moore graph, but its adjacency
multiplicities are nonintegral.  For diameter three,
Theorem~\ref{thm:lp-ceiling} gives \(n<19\).  The radius-two ball has
\(17\) vertices, and the third layer is nonempty, so \(n=18\).
Every vertex then has exactly one vertex at distance three: the
distance-three matrix is a perfect matching.
Its \(-1\)-eigenspace \(W=\ker(A_3+I)\) is a nine-dimensional rational
subspace of \(\one^\perp\).  Since \(A\) commutes with \(A_3\), the space
\(W\) is \(A\)-invariant, and
\[
 A_3=J+3I-A-A^2
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 (A^2+A-4I)|_W=0.
\]
The polynomial \(x^2+x-4\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\), so a rational
space on which it annihilates an operator has even dimension, a contradiction.

For \(k=5\), diameter two again fails the Moore multiplicity condition.
Diameter four can be excluded directly: a diametral geodesic gives the
principal submatrix \(D(P_5)\), and its factor \(x^2+6x+4\) supplies
the eigenvalue \(-3-\sqrt5<-5\).  Cauchy interlacing therefore contradicts
strictness.  In diameter three, Meringer's enumeration
\cite[p.~142]{Meringer1999} and Theorem~\ref{thm:lp-ceiling} leave
\(n\in\{30,31,32\}\).  The handshake identity \(5n=2|E(G)|\) gives
\(n\equiv0\pmod2\), leaving \(n\in\{30,32\}\).  At \(n=32\), compress the adjacency matrix to the distance layers about
a vertex.  Their sizes are \(1,5,20,6\).  Write \(a\) for the average
internal degree in the distance-two layer.  Each vertex of that layer has
one neighbour in the first layer, so the edges from the second to the third
layer satisfy \(20(4-a)\le6\cdot5\), giving \(a\ge5/2\).
On the normalized layer indicators, the symmetric compression is
\[
 Q(a)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 0&\sqrt5&0&0\\
 \sqrt5&0&2&0\\
 0&2&a&(4-a)\sqrt{10/3}\\
 0&0&(4-a)\sqrt{10/3}&5-\frac{10}{3}(4-a)
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The derivative of its only \(a\)-dependent block is
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}1&-\sqrt{10/3}\\-\sqrt{10/3}&10/3\end{pmatrix}
 =
 \begin{pmatrix}1\\-\sqrt{10/3}\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}1&-\sqrt{10/3}\end{pmatrix}\succeq0.
\]
This positive-semidefinite derivative makes every ordered eigenvalue of
\(Q(a)\) nondecreasing in \(a\).  Write
\(5=\mu_1(a)\ge\mu_2(a)\ge\mu_3(a)\ge\mu_4(a)\).
It is therefore enough to bound the second eigenvalue at the smallest
feasible value,
\[
 \chi_{Q(5/2)}(x)=\frac14(x-5)p_{5,6}(x),
 \qquad
 p_{5,6}(x)=4x^3+10x^2-16x-30,
 \qquad p_{5,6}(11/6)=-29/27<0.
\]
Since \(p_{5,6}(11/6)<0\) and \(p_{5,6}(x)\to+\infty\) as
\(x\to+\infty\), its largest root satisfies
\(\mu_2(5/2)>11/6>-1+\sqrt8\).  Monotonicity and Poincaré separation give
\[
 \theta_2(A)\ge\mu_2(a)\ge\mu_2(5/2),
\]
contradicting the necessary bound \(\theta_2(A)<-1+\sqrt8\).  At \(n=30\),
Meringer's isomorph-free enumeration leaves exactly four
\((5,5)\)-cages \cite[p.~142]{Meringer1999}; each fixed record has an exact
distance eigenvalue at most \(-5\). The characteristic-polynomial factors and their
roots are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:small-example-certificates};
the classification supplies exhaustion, while the factors supply the
distance-spectral obstruction.
The accompanying release contains an independent exact audit of the complete
case split and the four fixed graph6 records.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Endpoint-neighbourhood obstruction]\label{thm:endpoint-diameter}
Let \(G\) be any connected finite simple graph, and let \(u,v\) be vertices at
distance \(\ell=d_G(u,v)\ge5\).  Put \(p=d(u)\) and \(q=d(v)\).  Then
\[
 \resultbox{
 \lambda_{\min}(D(G))
 \le p+q-2-\sqrt{(p-q)^2+pq(\ell-2)^2}.
 }
\]
If \(\delta\) is the ordinary minimum degree, then
\[
 \resultbox{
 \lambda_{\min}(D(G))\le-\delta(\ell-4)-2.
 }
\]
Consequently every strict WOW-284 counterexample satisfies
\[
 \Delta>\delta(\ell-4)+2,
\]
where \(\Delta\) is the ordinary maximum degree.
In particular, every regular strict counterexample has diameter at most four.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We use opposite signs on the two endpoint neighbourhoods.  They are
disjoint, so give weight \(a>0\) to \(N(u)\), weight \(-b<0\) to
\(N(v)\), and zero elsewhere.  Distances within a neighbourhood are at
most two; distances between the neighbourhoods are at least \(\ell-2\).
The latter enter with negative products, so these upper and lower distance
bounds both give an upper bound on the Rayleigh quotient.  After normalizing
the neighbourhood indicators, this is the quotient of
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}
 2(p-1)&-(\ell-2)\sqrt{pq}\\
 -(\ell-2)\sqrt{pq}&2(q-1)
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The least eigenvalue gives the first bound.  Its eigenvector can be chosen
with both coordinates positive, as required by our sign assignment.
To replace the endpoint degrees by the minimum degree, write
\(p=\delta+\alpha\), \(q=\delta+\beta\), with
\(\alpha,\beta\ge0\), and put \(t=\ell-2\).  The right-hand side of the identity
\[
 (p-q)^2+pqt^2-(p+q+\delta(t-2))^2
 =(t-2)\{\delta t(\alpha+\beta)+(t+2)\alpha\beta\}
\]
is nonnegative.  Since \(p+q+\delta(t-2)>0\), comparison of the nonnegative
square roots gives the second bound.  Finally \(\delta^*(G)\le\Delta\).
The sign choice, radical comparison, and integer rounding are independently
audited by \codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_10_endpoint_diameter.py}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Diameter four]\label{thm:diameter-four}
Let \(G\) be connected, \(k\)-regular, of girth at least five and diameter
four.  Then
\[
 \resultbox{
 \lambda_{\min}(D(G))\le-\frac{7+\sqrt{16k+1}}2.
 }
\]
Consequently no such graph of degree \(2\le k\le9\) is a strict
counterexample.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose \(u,v\) at distance four and put \(U=N(u)\), \(V=N(v)\).  For fixed
\(a\in U\), every \(b\in V\) with \(d_G(a,b)=2\) has a common neighbour in
\(N(a)\setminus\{u\}\).  Distinct such vertices \(b,b'\) require distinct
common neighbours, since otherwise \(b-c-b'-v-b\) is a \(4\)-cycle.  Thus at
most \(k-1\) vertices of \(V\) are at distance two from each \(a\).  No pair
in \(U\times V\) is adjacent, and every remaining pair has distance at least
three.  Therefore
\[
 \sum_{a\in U,b\in V}d_G(a,b)\ge2k^2+k.
\]
The extra cross-distance contribution improves the Rayleigh bound.
For \(\alpha,\beta>0\), assign weights \(\alpha,\beta,-\alpha,-\beta\)
to \(u,U,v,V\), respectively.  Count unordered pairs with these weights
and double their contribution.  The result is
\[
 \frac{x^{\mathsf T}D(G)x}{x^{\mathsf T}x}
 \le
 \frac{-4\alpha^2-4k\alpha\beta-3k\beta^2}
 {\alpha^2+k\beta^2}.
\]
After setting \(y_1=\alpha\) and \(y_2=\sqrt{k}\,\beta\), the
right-hand side is the Rayleigh quotient of
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}-4&-2\sqrt{k}\\-2\sqrt{k}&-3\end{pmatrix},
\]
whose least eigenvalue is the displayed value and has a positive-coordinate
minimizer.  The strict comparison with \(-k\) holds for \(2\le k\le9\).
Every orientation factor, cross-distance sign, and endpoint comparison is
independently checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_11_diameter_four.py}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Regular trichotomy]\label{cor:trichotomy}
Every regular strict counterexample has one of the following forms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item diameter two, hence a Moore graph;
\item diameter three, with
\(\lvert\theta+1\rvert<\sqrt{2k-2}\) for every nonprincipal adjacency
eigenvalue \(\theta\);
\item diameter four, with degree at least ten.
\end{enumerate}
There are no regular strict counterexamples of diameter at least five.
\end{corollary}

\section{Moment bounds and the optimal polynomial order bound}\label{sec:lp}

Let \(G\) satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:diameter-three-score}, and
write its nonprincipal adjacency eigenvalues as \(\theta_i\).  Put
\(y_i=\theta_i+1\).

\begin{proposition}[Fourth-moment identity]\label{prop:moment-bound}
One has
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(2k-2-y_i^2)(y_i+1)^2
 =(k+2)\bigl((k+2)(k^2+3)-6n\bigr).
\]
Every strict counterexample therefore satisfies
\[
 \resultbox{
 n< B_k:=\frac{(k+2)(k^2+3)}6.
 }
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The fourth moment supplies an order bound because the shifted WOW window
makes every summand nonnegative.  The identity follows from
\[
 \tr A=\tr A^3=0,
 \quad \tr A^2=nk,
 \quad \tr A^4=nk(2k-1),
\]
after removing the principal eigenvalue and expanding both sides.
In a strict counterexample, each factor \(2k-2-y_i^2\) is positive.
To obtain a strict order bound, we must also exclude a zero sum: otherwise every nonprincipal adjacency eigenvalue would equal
\(-2\), and \(\tr A=0\) would give
\(k-2(n-1)=0\), or \(n=(k+2)/2\), incompatible with the elementary
bound \(n\ge k+1\) for a simple \(k\)-regular graph.  The identity is
checked symbolically by
\codefile{scripts/verify_degree_six_gate.py} and independently within
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_02_two_sided_lp.py}.
\end{proof}

The fourth-moment argument uses a quartic polynomial.
We now allow higher degrees and minimize the order bound over polynomials
satisfying the coefficient and sign conditions below.
This is the one-variable nonbacktracking linear program.
We exhibit a polynomial attaining its optimum and a dual measure proving
that no polynomial in this class gives a smaller bound.
The same measure determines the equality cases.

\begin{definition}
Let \(F_i=F_i^{(k)}\) be the nonbacktracking polynomials from
Proposition~\ref{prop:higher-transfer}, and set
\[
 I_k=[-1-\sqrt{2k-2},-1+\sqrt{2k-2}].
\]
A finite polynomial \(f=\sum_i f_iF_i\) is \emph{admissible} if
\[
 f_0>0,
 \qquad f_i\ge0\quad(i\ge5),
 \qquad f(x)\le0\quad(x\in I_k).
\]
\end{definition}

For a \(k\)-regular graph of girth at least five whose nonprincipal spectrum
lies in \(I_k\), one has \(\tr F_i(A)=0\) for \(1\le i\le4\), while
\(\tr F_i(A)\ge0\) for \(i\ge5\), since these traces count closed
nonbacktracking walks \cite[Theorem~1 and Corollary~1]{Nozaki2015}.
The coefficient conditions therefore give
\(nf_0\le\tr f(A)\).  On the other hand, \(f(\theta)\le0\) for every
nonprincipal eigenvalue, so \(\tr f(A)\le f(k)\).  Thus
\[
 nf_0\le\tr f(A)\le f(k),
 \qquad n\le\frac{f(k)}{f_0}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Exact LP ceiling and rigidity]\label{thm:lp-ceiling}
For every integer \(k\ge4\) and every admissible \(f\),
\[
 \resultbox{
 \frac{f(k)}{f_0}\ge B_k=\frac{(k+2)(k^2+3)}6.
 }
\]
Equality holds if and only if \(f\) is a positive scalar multiple of
\[
 \resultbox{
 f_*(x)=\frac{(x+2)^2(x^2+2x-(2k-3))}{6(k+2)}.
 }
\]
Thus increasing the polynomial degree cannot improve this one-variable LP
bound.  Consequently, any connected \(k\)-regular graph of girth at least
five whose nonprincipal spectrum lies in \(I_k\) satisfies \(n<B_k\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We first check that the stated quartic attains the proposed bound.
Its nonbacktracking expansion is
\[
\begin{aligned}
6(k+2)f_*(x)={}&6(k+2)F_0(x)+2(2k+7)F_1(x)\\
&+(k+13)F_2(x)+6F_3(x)+F_4(x).
\end{aligned}
\]
The constant coefficient is positive, and there are no coefficients above
degree four.  On \(I_k\), the factor \((x+1)^2-(2k-2)\) is nonpositive, while
\((x+2)^2\ge0\); hence \(f_*\) is admissible and \(f_*(k)=B_k\).

To prove that no admissible polynomial does better, we use a positive
measure supported on the two window endpoints and the interior zero of the
quartic.  Put \(\Delta=\sqrt{2k-2}\),
\(\xi_\pm=-1\pm\Delta\), and \(\xi_0=-2\), and define
\begin{align*}
w_-&=\frac{k(k+2)(2k^2-6-3(k-1)\Delta)}{24(2k-3)},\\
w_0&=\frac{k(k-1)(k^2+3)}{6(2k-3)},\\
w_+&=\frac{k(k+2)(2k^2-6+3(k-1)\Delta)}{24(2k-3)}.
\end{align*}
\(w_-,w_0,w_+>0\).  For the only nontrivial inequality, this follows from
\[
 (2k^2-6)^2-18(k-1)^3
 =2(k-3)(2k-3)(k^2+3)>0.
\]
The measure
\(\mu=w_-\delta_{\xi_-}+w_0\delta_{\xi_0}+w_+\delta_{\xi_+}\) satisfies
\[
 \mu(1)=B_k-1,
 \qquad \mu(F_i)=-F_i(k)\quad(1\le i\le4).
\]
The measure cancels the first four nonconstant basis terms.  For the
remaining terms, we need the slack \(a_i=\mu(F_i)+F_i(k)\) to be strictly
positive for \(i\ge5\).  Appendix~\ref{app:lp-slack-positivity} verifies this positivity: the first
five values follow from exact polynomial expansions, and a Chebyshev bound
handles every later term.  With this calculation available, the optimality
and rigidity argument is short.
We can now compare an arbitrary admissible polynomial with the quartic.
Expansion in the nonbacktracking basis gives
\[
 \int f\,d\mu
 =B_kf_0-f(k)+\sum_{i\ge5}f_i a_i
 \ge B_kf_0-f(k).
\]
The last sum is nonnegative by the coefficient condition and the slack
positivity verified in Appendix~\ref{app:lp-slack-positivity}.  On the other hand,
\(f\le0\) on \(\operatorname{supp}\mu\), so \(\int f\,d\mu\le0\).
These inequalities prove the lower bound for every admissible polynomial.
Equality must hold in both inequalities.  Since every high-degree slack
is strictly positive, \(f_i=0\) for \(i\ge5\).  The measure has positive
weight at each support point, so \(f\) must vanish at
\(\xi_-,-2,\xi_+\).  Its interior zero \(-2\) has even multiplicity,
because \(f\le0\) on \(I_k\).  A polynomial of degree at most four with
these zeros is a scalar multiple of \(f_*\), and \(f_0>0\) makes that
scalar positive.  The polynomial bound allows the endpoints of \(I_k\).  We now show
that no graph attains this bound.  Suppose \(n=B_k\).  Equality in the
trace bound for the optimal quartic forces every nonprincipal eigenvalue
to belong to
\[
 \{-2,-1-\Delta,-1+\Delta\},\qquad \Delta=\sqrt{2k-2}.
\]
Let \(m_0,m_-,m_+\) be their respective multiplicities.  If
\(\Delta\) is irrational, the trace equation
\[
 0=k-(m_-+m_+)-2m_0+\Delta(m_+-m_-)
\]
forces \(m_+=m_-\).  Counting the nonprincipal eigenvalues then gives
\(m_0=k-n+1<0\), since
\[
 B_k-(k+1)=\frac{k(k-1)(k+3)}6>0.
\]
This is impossible.  Thus \(\Delta\) is rational and, as the square root
of an integer, must be an integer.

The second moment of \(A+I\) now determines \(m_0\).  The principal
eigenvalue contributes \((k+1)^2\).  Each nonprincipal adjacency
eigenvalue \(-1\pm\Delta\) contributes \(2k-2\) to this moment,
while each copy of \(-2\) contributes \(1\).  Hence
\[
 (2k-2)(n-1-m_0)+m_0=n(k+1)-(k+1)^2,
\]
and substituting \(n=B_k\) gives
\[
 m_0=\frac{k(k-1)(k^2+3)}{6(2k-3)}.
\]
Since \(m_0\) is an integer, \(2k-3\) divides
\(k(k-1)(k^2+3)\).  The identity
\[
 16k(k-1)(k^2+3)
 =(2k-3)(8k^3+4k^2+30k+21)+63
\]
therefore implies \(2k-3\mid63\).  With \(k\ge4\), this leaves
\(k\in\{5,6,12,33\}\).  Only \(k=33\) has integral
\(\sqrt{2k-2}\), but then \(m_0=9152/3\), again impossible.
Consequently \(n<B_k\) throughout the closed window.
The LP certificate identities, finite slacks, tail bound, and
polynomial equality calculation are independently checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_02_two_sided_lp.py}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Integral optimal-slack bound]\label{thm:integral-slack}
Let \(G\) be connected and \(k\)-regular with \(k\ge4\), of girth at least
five and diameter three, and suppose
\[
 \lvert\theta+1\rvert<\sqrt{2k-2}
 \qquad(\theta\ne k)
\]
for every adjacency eigenvalue \(\theta\).  Define
\[
 g_k(x)=(x+2)^2\bigl((x+1)^2-(2k-2)\bigr),\quad
 C_k=(k+2)^2(k^2+3),\quad h_k=6(k+2),
\]
\[
 \mathcal S_k=-g_k(A)+\frac{C_k}{n}J.
\]
Then
\[
 \mathcal S_k\succeq0,\qquad
 \mathcal S_k\one=0,\qquad
 \tr\mathcal S_k=h_k(B_k-n).
\]
Moreover,
\[
 \mathcal E_k=g_k(A)-(h_k+1)J+I
\]
is a nonzero, symmetric, entrywise nonnegative integral matrix with zero
diagonal and constant row sum
\[
 \varepsilon_{k,n}=C_k-(h_k+1)n+1.
\]
On \(\one^\perp\), one has \(\mathcal S_k=I-\mathcal E_k\).  In particular,
\[
 \resultbox{
 n\le
 \left\lfloor
 \frac{(k+2)^2(k^2+3)}{6(k+2)+1}
 \right\rfloor.
 }
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We apply the optimal LP polynomial \(g_k=6(k+2)f_*\) from
Theorem~\ref{thm:lp-ceiling} to the adjacency matrix.  The resulting
\(\mathcal S_k\) vanishes on \(\langle\one\rangle\).  On a nonprincipal
\(\theta\)-eigenspace, its eigenvalue is
\[
 \bigl(2k-2-(\theta+1)^2\bigr)(\theta+2)^2\ge0.
\]
To compute the diagonal, use the nonbacktracking expansion of \(g_k\),
whose constant coefficient is \(h_k\).  Girth at least five implies
\((F_i(A))_{uu}=0\) for every \(u\) and \(1\le i\le4\).  Thus
\[
 \tr\mathcal S_k=C_k-h_kn=h_k(B_k-n),
 \qquad (g_k(A))_{uu}=h_k.
\]

The off-diagonal entries now supply the extra restriction.  Put
\(z_{uv}=(g_k(A))_{uv}\in\mathbb Z\) for \(u\ne v\), and set
\(a=C_k/n-h_k=(\mathcal S_k)_{uu}\).  Positivity of the \(2\times2\)
principal minor on \(\{u,v\}\) gives
\[
 \left|\frac{C_k}{n}-z_{uv}\right|\le a.
\]
We must exclude \(C_k/n-z_{uv}=a\), equivalently \(z_{uv}=h_k\). This would imply
\((e_u-e_v)^{\mathsf T}\mathcal S_k(e_u-e_v)=0\), and positivity would then
put \(e_u-e_v\in\ker\mathcal S_k\). Write \(E_{-2}(A)=\ker(A+2I)\). The strict spectral window gives
\[
 \ker\mathcal S_k=\langle\one\rangle\oplus E_{-2}(A).
\]
Since \(e_u-e_v\perp\one\), it would be a \((-2)\)-eigenvector of \(A\).
Yet the \(u\)-coordinate of \(A(e_u-e_v)\) is \(-1\) if \(u\sim v\) and
\(0\) otherwise, never \(-2\).  Therefore
\[
 z_{uv}\ge h_k+1.
\]
This proves the stated entrywise properties of \(\mathcal E_k\).
Its row sum follows from \(g_k(A)\one=C_k\one\); on \(\one^\perp\),
the term \(J\) vanishes and gives the claimed identity.

The remaining issue is \(\mathcal E_k\ne0\).  If this fails, then
\[
 g_k(A)=(h_k+1)J-I,
\]
so \(p_k(A)=0\) on \(\one^\perp\), where \(p_k(x)=g_k(x)+1\).  After
\(y=x+2\),
\[
 p_k(y-2)=y^4-2y^3+(3-2k)y^2+1.
\]
There are two possible ways to factor this polynomial over \(\mathbb Q\).
A rational linear factor would give a root among \(\pm1\), whose values are
\(3-2k\) and \(7-2k\).  For a factorization into monic integer quadratics,
the constant terms would be both \(1\) or both \(-1\).  The cubic and
linear coefficients would then be equal or opposite, respectively; their
actual values are \(-2\) and \(0\).  Neither factorization is possible.

The adjacency operator on the rational zero-sum space is annihilated by
this irreducible quartic.  Its characteristic polynomial must therefore
be a power of that quartic:
\[
 \chi_{A|_{\one^\perp}}(x)=p_k(x)^m,\qquad n-1=4m.
\]
The four roots of \(p_k\) sum to \(-6\), so \(\tr A=0\) gives
\[
 0=k-6m.
\]
Hence \(n-1=4m=2k/3\), contradicting \(n\ge k+1\).  Thus
\(\mathcal E_k\ne0\).  Its constant row sum is consequently a positive
integer, so \(\varepsilon_{k,n}\ge1\), which is equivalent to the displayed
order bound.  The symbolic expansion, irreducibility alternatives, and finite
specializations have also been checked by independent exact audits.
\end{proof}

\section{Integral excess, order bounds, and local cycle counts}
\label{sec:degree-six}

The trace of \(\mathcal S_k\), apart from a positive factor,
is the gap between the graph order and the scalar LP bound.
We now use the entries as well as the trace.
Its \(2\times2\) principal minors constrain individual entries,
and larger minors relate several entries.  In the associated integral
excess matrix, these constraints become arithmetic restrictions.
They first give the three-to-one excess bound.
Applied to individual edges, they also turn local cycle counts into
obstructions that the scalar order bound does not see.

\subsection{The three-to-one excess bound}

\begin{theorem}[Three-to-one excess bound]\label{thm:three-to-one}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:integral-slack}, assume \(k\ge6\)
and write \(\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{k,n}\).  The integral parameter
\[
 r=2\varepsilon-n-2
   =2(k+2)^2(k^2+3)-(12k+27)n
\]
satisfies
\[
 r>0,\qquad n\le3r.
\]
Consequently
\[
 \resultbox{
 n\le\left\lfloor
 \frac{3(k+2)^2(k^2+3)}{18k+41}
 \right\rfloor.
 }
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put \(E=\mathcal E_k\), \(C=C_k\), \(h=h_k\), and
\[
 \rho=\frac{\varepsilon-1}{n}
      =\frac{1+r/n}{2}.
\]
The optimal slack matrix has the form
\[
 \mathcal S_k=I-E+\rho J\succeq0.
\]
Replace each edge of a graph by a vertex and join two of these vertices
when their edges meet. The resulting graph is its line graph. A complete
graph with a perfect matching removed is called a cocktail-party graph.

The proof has two stages.  We first assume \(r\le0\), show that \(E\)
is the adjacency matrix of a simple graph, and rule out its complement
using the least-eigenvalue classification and rational eigenspace dimensions.
Once \(r>0\) is known, we assume \(n>3r\).  We then exclude four cases,
in order:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an entry \(E_{uv}=2\), which forces \(n=3r+1\) or \(3r+2\)
and is excluded by divisibility and parity;
\item a simple \(E\) whose complement has at least three components,
which forces \(n\le3r+6\) and is excluded by fixed remainders;
\item a connected complement, whose cocktail-party case contradicts
\(r>0\) and whose line-graph roots are excluded by size and divisibility;
\item a complement with two components, excluded by their classified types
and the remaining fixed remainders.
\end{enumerate}
Here diameter three and the radius-two count give
\(n\ge k^2+2\ge38\).  The classification therefore applies whenever the
complement is connected.  In the final two-component case we first exclude
\(k=6,7,8\); for \(k\ge9\), each component has order greater than \(28\).
Before starting these cases, we record the arithmetic restrictions used
to close them.  The first comes from the identity
\[
 128(2C-r)
 =(4k+9)(64k^3+112k^2+196k+327)+(129-128r).
\]
Since \(n=(2C-r)/(12k+27)\) is integral and \(4k+9\) is odd,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r-divisibility}
 4k+9\mid129-128r.
\end{equation}
The second condition will be used when the order is close to the desired
bound.  If \(n=3r+t\), the defining equation for \(r\) gives
\[
 18k+41\mid 2C-(12k+27)t.
\]
Since \(\gcd(18,18k+41)=1\), Euclidean division after multiplication by
\(18^4\) yields the necessary condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-remainder}
 18k+41\mid 132650+34992t.
\end{equation}

We first prove positivity of \(r\).  Suppose \(r\le0\).  A
\(2\times2\) principal minor of \(\mathcal S_k\) gives
\[
 E_{uv}\le2+\frac rn\qquad(u\ne v).
\]
Thus \(E\) is the adjacency matrix of a simple graph when \(r<0\).  If
\(r=0\) and \(E_{uv}=2\), positivity puts \(e_u+e_v\) in
\(\ker\mathcal S_k\).  After subtracting its projection onto \(\one\), the
strict spectral window gives a \((-2)\)-eigenvector of \(A\).  Its
\(u\)-coordinate would require
\[
 A_{uv}=-2+\frac{2k+4}{n},
\]
which is impossible because \(n\ge k^2+2\) and \(A_{uv}\in\{0,1\}\).
Hence \(E\) is simple also when \(r=0\).

Let \(X\) be the graph with adjacency matrix \(J-I-E\).  It is regular of degree
\[
 d=n-1-\varepsilon=\frac{n-r-4}{2},
\]
and \(A(X)=-I-E+J\) has least eigenvalue at least \(-2\).  Since each
component has at least \(d+1\) vertices, \(X\) has at most two components.
The exceptional possibilities will be tested on eigenspaces of \(E\).
Because this matrix is a polynomial in \(A\) and \(J\), it commutes with
\(A\), so each rational eigenspace of \(E\) is \(A\)-invariant.

The graph \(X\) is now simple and regular, and its order is
\(n\ge38\).  If it is connected, the classification of connected regular
graphs of order greater than \(28\) and least eigenvalue at least
\(-2\) makes it a line graph or a cocktail-party graph
\cite{CameronEtAl1976}; see also
\cite[Theorems~4.1.1 and~4.1.5]{CvetkovicEtAl2004} and
\cite[Introduction]{KoolenEtAl2025}.

Suppose first that \(X\) is connected.  The complete case would give
\(E=0\), excluded in Theorem~\ref{thm:integral-slack}.  In the
cocktail-party case, \(E\) is a perfect matching and \(r=-n\).  The identity
\[
 1296C=(6k+13)(216k^3+396k^2+654k+1175)+277
\]
forces \(k=44\) and \(n=14812\).  On the \(7406\)-dimensional
 \((-1)\)-eigenspace of \(E\), the rational operator \(A\) is annihilated by
 \(g_{44}(x)+2\).

If \(X=L(Y)\), choose a connected root \(Y\). Since \(X\) is
\(d\)-regular, every edge \(ab\) of \(Y\) satisfies
\(\deg_Y(a)+\deg_Y(b)=d+2\). Degrees alternate along paths. An odd
cycle forces them to coincide; otherwise \(Y\) is bipartite, with a
constant degree on each part. Thus \(Y\) is regular or semiregular
bipartite. Here semiregular means that the degree is constant within each
part, though the two degrees may differ.

If \(Y\) is \(q\)-regular, then \(q\ge n/4\) and
\(|V(Y)|\le8\), contradicting \(q\le |V(Y)|-1\) because \(n\ge38\).
If \(Y\) is semiregular bipartite with part sizes \(a\ge b\ge2\), then
\[
 \frac1a+\frac1b=\frac{n-r}{2n}\ge\frac12,\qquad n\le ab.
\]
The cases \(b\ge3\) have \(ab<38\).  For \(b=2\), connectedness forces
\(Y=K_{a,2}\), hence \(r=-4\).  Equation~\eqref{eq:r-divisibility} leaves
\(k=158\) and \(n=664748\).  The resulting \((-1)\)-eigenspace of \(E\)
 has dimension \(332373\).

If \(X\) has two components, write their orders as
\(d+1+a_1\) and \(d+1+a_2\).  Then
\[
 a_1+a_2=r+2,
\]
so \(r\in\{-2,-1,0\}\).  Checking the divisors in
\eqref{eq:r-divisibility}, together with \(n\ge k^2+2\) and \(kn\) even,
leaves only
\[
 (r,k,n)=(-1,62,40875).
\]
The components then have orders \(20437\) and \(20438\).  Their common
degree is one less than the smaller order, so the smaller component is
complete.  In the larger component, each vertex has exactly one
nonneighbour; these missing edges form a perfect matching, giving the
cocktail-party graph.  Thus \(E\) has eigenvalue \(-1\) with
 multiplicity \(10219\).

We exclude all three exceptional cases using the eigenspaces just found.
These rational spaces are orthogonal to the constant vector and invariant
under the adjacency operator.  On each of them the excess matrix has
eigenvalue minus one, so its defining polynomial relation gives the
annihilator \(g_k(x)+2\).  This quartic is irreducible over
\(\mathbb Q\) for every integer \(k\ge6\), except \(k=7\).  To check
irreducibility, substitute \(y=x+2\), giving
 \[
  y^4-2y^3+(3-2k)y^2+2.
 \]
 Gauss's lemma reduces a quadratic factorization to monic integer quadratics
 whose constant terms multiply to \(2\); coefficient comparison leaves only
 \(k=4\) or \(k=7\), while the rational-root alternatives give \(k=2\) or
 \(k=4\).  The degrees \(44,62,158\) are therefore irreducible cases, but the
 corresponding dimensions \(7406,10219,332373\) are not divisible by four.
 Since an invariant rational space annihilated by an irreducible quartic is a
 vector space over the degree-four field \(\mathbb Q[x]/(g_k(x)+2)\), its
 rational dimension must be divisible by four.
 This contradiction proves \(r>0\).

We now assume, for a contradiction, that \(n>3r\), and put
\(x=r/n\in(0,1/3)\).  Before applying the graph classification again,
we show that the excess matrix is simple.  The \(2\times2\) minors give
\(E_{uv}\le2\).  Suppose \(E_{uv}=2\), and for
\(w\notin\{u,v\}\) set \(s_w=E_{uw}+E_{vw}\).  Cauchy--Schwarz,
applied to \(e_u+e_v\) and \(e_w\) in the Gram matrix
\(\mathcal S_k\), gives
\[
 (1+x-s_w)^2\le x(3+x),
\]
Thus the nonnegative integer \(s_w\) belongs to \(\{1,2\}\).  Summing
over the rows shows that exactly \(r\) vertices satisfy \(s_w=2\).
We collect these vertices in \(W\), let \(y=\sum_{w\in W}e_w\), and put
\[
  e_W=\sum_{\{w,z\}\subseteq W}E_{wz}.
\]
 Then
\[
 \begin{aligned}
  (e_u+e_v)^{\mathsf T}\mathcal S_k(e_u+e_v)&=2x,\\
  (e_u+e_v)^{\mathsf T}\mathcal S_k y&=r(x-1),\\
  y^{\mathsf T}\mathcal S_k y&=r+\frac{r^2(1+x)}2-2e_W.
 \end{aligned}
\]
 Positivity of this \(2\times2\) Gram determinant yields
 \[
  2xr+r^2(3x-1)-4xe_W\ge0.
 \]
Since \(e_W\ge0\), this forces \(n\le3r+2\).  Our contrary assumption
therefore leaves only \(n=3r+t\) with \(t\in\{1,2\}\).
Equation~\eqref{eq:fixed-remainder} reduces these two possibilities to:
 \[
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
  t&\text{remainder}&\text{admissible }(18k+41,k)\\
  \hline
  1&167642&\text{none}\\
  2&202634&(1427,77).
 \end{array}
 \]
 The remaining case gives \((k,n,r)=(77,77831,25943)\), but \(kn\) is odd,
 contrary to the handshake lemma.  Therefore \(E\) is simple.

We may therefore again treat \(X\), with adjacency matrix \(J-I-E\),
as a simple graph.  It is \(d\)-regular and has least eigenvalue at least
\(-2\).  If \(X\) had three or more components, then \(n\ge3(d+1)\), or
\(n\le3r+6\).  This leaves \(n=3r+t\) for \(1\le t\le6\), and
Equation~\eqref{eq:fixed-remainder} gives
 \[
 \begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
  t&1&2&3&4&5&6\\ \hline
  \text{remainder}
   &167642&202634&237626&272618&307610&342602,
 \end{array}
 \]
 and leaves no integral graph: the \(t=2\) case is excluded above, while the
 sole divisor candidate for \(t=3\) does not make \(r\) integral.  Hence \(X\)
 has at most two components.

For connected \(X\), the cocktail-party case contradicts \(r>0\).
We are left with \(X=L(Y)\), with the same regular/semiregular
dichotomy as above. If \(Y\) is \(q\)-regular on \(v\) vertices, then
 \[
  q=\frac{n-r}{4}>\frac n6,\qquad v=\frac{2n}{q}<12.
 \]
 Simplicity and \(n\ge38\) leave only
 \[
 \begin{aligned}
  (q,v;n,r)\in\{&(8,10;40,8),(9,10;45,9),\\
                 &(8,11;44,12),(10,11;55,15)\}.
 \end{aligned}
 \]
 The radius-two lower bound forces \(k\le7\), and direct substitution in the
 defining formula for \(r\) excludes all four cases.

 For a semiregular bipartite root with part sizes \(a\ge b\ge2\),
 write \(p=n/a\le q=n/b\) for its two degrees.  Then
 \[
  p+q=\frac{n-r}{2}>\frac n3,
  \qquad (b-2)n=b(r+2p),
 \]
 so \(b<6\).  Connectedness excludes \(p=1\).  For \(b=2\), the
 left side of the displayed identity is zero while the right side is
 positive.  For \(b=5\), the degree-sum inequality makes the sum of the
 reciprocals of the part sizes greater than one third.  The larger part
 therefore has at most seven vertices, and the root has at most thirty-five
 edges, below the required line-graph order thirty-eight.  The case \(b=4\) leaves only
 \[
  (p,q,a,b;n,r)=(4,10,10,4;40,12),\ (4,11,11,4;44,14),
 \]
 both excluded by the radius-two bound and the formula for \(r\).
 For \(b=3\), one has \(p=2\) or \(3\), giving \(n=3r+12\) or
 \(n=3r+18\).  Equation~\eqref{eq:fixed-remainder} gives the remainders
 \(552554=2\cdot276277\) and \(762506=2\cdot381253\).  Their odd cofactors
 are prime and congruent to \(13\pmod {18}\), whereas \(18k+41\equiv5
 \pmod {18}\).  Thus \(X\) cannot be connected.

Only the case of two components remains.  When \(k=6,7,8\), the
inequalities \(r>0\) and \(n>3r\) leave no admissible integral order.
For \(k\ge9\), we have \(n>150\), so both components have order greater
than \(28\) and the classification applies to each.  Suppose a component
of order \(N\) is the line graph of a simple \(q\)-regular graph \(Y\)
on \(v\) vertices.  Its degree is \(d=2q-2\), and the number of edges
of the root is \(N=vq/2\).  Hence
\[
 q=\frac{d+2}{2}=\frac{n-r}{4}>\frac n6>25,
 \qquad
 v=\frac{2N}{q}\le\frac{2n}{q}<12.
\]
Simplicity gives \(q\le v-1\le10\), a contradiction.  If a component
of order \(N\) has a semiregular bipartite root, its part sizes satisfy
 \[
  \frac1a+\frac1b=\frac{d+2}{N}
  \ge\frac{n-r}{n+r+2}>\frac{49}{100}.
 \]
 If \(b\ge3\), the five possible pairs \((a,b)\) have \(ab\le18<N\).
 If \(b=2\), connectedness forces the root \(K_{d,2}\), whose line graph has
 order \(2d\).  Thus every component is \(K_{d+1}\), a cocktail-party graph
 of order \(d+2\), or \(L(K_{d,2})\).  The first two types alone force
 \(r\le0\).  If exactly one component has order \(2d\), then
\(n=3r+8\) or \(n=3r+10\). If both do, then \(n=2r+8\). Together with \(n>3r\), this gives \(r<8\), hence \(n<24\),
  contrary to \(n>150\). For the two remaining mixed cases,
  the divisor checks in Appendix~\ref{app:three-to-one-arithmetic} exclude
  \(n=3r+8\) and leave only \(k=123\) for \(n=3r+10\).
  In that case \(kn\) is odd. This final contradiction proves \(n\le3r\).

We have excluded every case under the contrary assumption.  Substituting
the definition of \(r\) into the resulting three-to-one inequality gives
 \[
  (18k+41)n\le3(k+2)^2(k^2+3),
 \]
 which is the displayed bound.  The quartic factorization alternatives, aggregate Gram
 determinant, and divisor and line-root cases used after \(r>0\) is
 established are checked in
 \codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_14_three_to_one.py}. The exceptional
 nonpositive-\(r\) parameter cases are also checked by
 \texttt{nonpositive\_r\_audit()} in
 \codefile{scripts/verify_three_to_one_excess_bound.py}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Rigidity at equality]\label{cor:three-to-one-equality}
Under the hypotheses and notation of Theorem~\ref{thm:three-to-one}, equality
in \(n\le3r\) can occur only for
\[
 \resultbox{(k,n,r)=(103,185220,61740).}
\]
This is an arithmetic parameter classification; it does not assert the
existence of a graph with these parameters.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Equality gives
\[
 (18k+41)r=(k+2)^2(k^2+3)=:C_k.
\]
Exact Euclidean division yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
18^4C_k={}&(18k+41)
 (5832k^3+10044k^2+17946k+29107)\\
&+66325,
\end{aligned}
\]
where \(66325=5^2\cdot7\cdot379\).  Hence \(18k+41\mid66325\).
Since \(k\ge6\), this divisor is at least \(149\) and is congruent to \(5\)
modulo \(18\).  Among the positive divisors of \(66325\), only
\(1895\) has these properties.  Thus \(k=103\), after which direct
substitution gives \(r=61740\) and \(n=3r=185220\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{A signed graph from the excess matrix}

\begin{proposition}[Signed-complement bridge]\label{prop:signed-complement}
Under the hypotheses and notation of Theorem~\ref{thm:three-to-one}, assume
\(0<r<n\) and define
\[
 S=(6k+14)J-2I-g_k(A).
\]
Then \(S\) is a signed adjacency matrix:
\[
 S_{uu}=0,\qquad S_{uv}\in\{-1,0,1\}\quad(u\ne v).
\]
It has constant signed row sum \((n-r-4)/2\), and
\[
 S+2I\succeq0,\qquad E_{-2}(S)=E_{-2}(A).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write \(x=r/n\in(0,1)\), \(\rho=(1+x)/2\), and
\(E=\mathcal E_k\).  Since
\[
 \mathcal S_k=I-E+\rho J\succeq0,
\]
its \(2\times2\) principal minors give
\(-1\le E_{uv}\le2+x\).  The entries of \(E\) are nonnegative integers, so
\(E_{uv}\in\{0,1,2\}\) for \(u\ne v\), and \(S=J-I-E\) has the asserted
entries.  The row sum follows from
\(\varepsilon=(n+r+2)/2\).  Moreover,
\[
 S+2I
 =\mathcal S_k+\frac{n-r}{2n}J\succeq0.
\]
It remains to identify the shared eigenspace.  The matrices \(A\) and
\(S\) commute; a nonprincipal adjacency eigenvector of eigenvalue
\(\theta\) has, under \(S\), eigenvalue \(-2-g_k(\theta)\).
Inside the open shifted WOW window, \(g_k\) vanishes only at
\(\theta=-2\), since its other zeros are the excluded endpoints.
The eigenvalue of \(S\) on the constant vector is
\((n-r-4)/2>-2\), since \(r<n\).
Thus the two \((-2)\)-eigenspaces coincide.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Five-cycle counts through an edge}

\begin{proposition}[Edge-local cycle bounds]\label{prop:edge-cycle}
Let \(G\) be connected and \(k\)-regular, where \(k\ge4\), of girth at least
five and diameter
three, and suppose
\[
 |\theta+1|\le\sqrt{2k-2}
 \qquad(\theta\ne k)
\]
for every adjacency eigenvalue \(\theta\).  For an edge \(uv\), let
\(\sigma_{uv}\) be the number of
\(5\)-cycles containing that edge.  Then
\[
 2k-2\le\sigma_{uv}\le
 \frac{2(k+2)^2(k^2+3)}n-10k-26.
\]
If \(n=k^2+1+c\), then also
\[
 \sigma_{uv}\ge(k-1)^2-c.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Recall \(g_k\) and \(C_k=g_k(k)\) from
Theorem~\ref{thm:integral-slack}, and put
\[
 M=-g_k(A)+\frac{C_k}{n}J.
\]
The spectral window gives \(M\succeq0\).  For an edge \(uv\),
\[
 (A^3)_{uv}=\sum_{z\sim v}(A^2)_{uz}=k+(k-1)=2k-1.
\]
Here \(z=u\) contributes \(k\), while every other neighbour of \(v\) is at
distance two from \(u\) and has a unique length-two path from \(u\).
Moreover,
\[
 (A^4)_{uv}=\sum_z(A^2)_{uz}(A^2)_{zv}=\sigma_{uv}.
\]
Indeed, the nonzero summands away from the diagonal are precisely the vertices
at distance two from both \(u\) and \(v\).  Their two unique length-two
paths, together with \(uv\), form a five-cycle, and each five-cycle through
\(uv\) yields one such vertex.  Since
\(C_k=(k+2)^2(k^2+3)\), the diagonal and edge entries of \(M\) are
\[
 a=\frac{C_k}{n}-6(k+2),
 \qquad
 b=\frac{C_k}{n}-(4k+14)-\sigma_{uv}.
\]
The principal submatrix on \(\{u,v\}\) is
\(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&a\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), so
\(a\ge0\) and \(-a\le b\le a\).  The inequality \(b\le a\) gives
\(\sigma_{uv}\ge2k-2\), while \(b\ge-a\) gives the stated upper bound.

For the final bound, every radius-two ball has size \(k^2+1\).  The set
\[
 \{u,v\}\cup(N(u)\setminus\{v\})\cup(N(v)\setminus\{u\})
\]
contains \(2k\) vertices and lies in \(B_2(u)\cap B_2(v)\).  Every further
intersection vertex is at distance two from both endpoints and is therefore in
the preceding five-cycle bijection.  Hence
\[
 |B_2(u)\cap B_2(v)|=2k+\sigma_{uv}.
\]
Inclusion--exclusion and \(n=k^2+1+c\) now give
\(\sigma_{uv}\ge(k-1)^2-c\).  The complete walk classification, sign
directions, and radius-two bijection have also been checked independently and
exactly.
\end{proof}

For \(\ell\ge3\), let \(N_\ell\) denote the number of
\(\ell\)-cycles in \(G\).

\begin{corollary}[Edge--cycle divisibility sieve]
\label{cor:edge-cycle-sieve}
Under the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:edge-cycle}, put
\[
 L_{k,n}=\max\{2k-2,\;2k^2-2k+2-n\},
 \qquad
 U_{k,n}=\frac{2(k+2)^2(k^2+3)}{n}-10k-26.
\]
Necessarily
\[
 \resultbox{\lceil L_{k,n}\rceil\le\lfloor U_{k,n}\rfloor.}
\]
If both sides equal an integer \(s\), then every edge of \(G\) lies in
exactly \(s\) five-cycles and
\[
 \resultbox{5\mid \frac{skn}{2}.}
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Writing \(n=k^2+1+c\), the two lower bounds in
Proposition~\ref{prop:edge-cycle} combine to
\[
 \sigma_{uv}\ge
 \max\{2k-2,(k-1)^2-c\}=L_{k,n},
\]
while its upper bound is \(U_{k,n}\).  Since \(\sigma_{uv}\) is an integer,
the first conclusion follows.  If the two integer bounds coincide at \(s\),
then \(\sigma_{uv}=s\) for every edge.  Counting edge--five-cycle incidences
gives
\[
 5N_5=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}\sigma_{uv}
 =s|E(G)|=\frac{skn}{2},
\]
which proves the divisibility condition.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Order bounds in degrees six through nine}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:degree-six-fifty}
Every connected \(6\)-regular strict counterexample to WOW-284 has order at
most \(50\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
To apply the order bound, we first reduce to diameter three.
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-diameter} excludes diameter at least five,
and Theorem~\ref{thm:diameter-four} excludes diameter four.  Diameter two would force equality in the
Moore bound, hence order \(37\) and adjacency characteristic polynomial
\((x-6)(x^2+x-5)^{18}\); its root sum is \(-12\), contradicting
\(\tr A=0\).  Diameter one is excluded by the girth hypothesis.  Thus the
diameter is three, and
 Theorem~\ref{thm:three-to-one} gives
\[
 n\le\left\lfloor\frac{3\cdot8^2\cdot39}{149}\right\rfloor=50.
\]

There is also a short local obstruction to the first excluded order.
If \(n=51\), Corollary~\ref{cor:edge-cycle-sieve} gives
\(\lceil L_{6,51}\rceil=\lfloor U_{6,51}\rfloor=11\), forcing
\[
 5N_5=153\cdot11=1683,
 \qquad 5\nmid 1683,
\]
which is impossible.  An independent exact audit verifies the full diameter
reduction and incidence calculation.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Low-degree order windows]\label{cor:low-degree-windows}
There is no regular strict counterexample of degree at most five.  In degrees
six through nine, every regular strict counterexample satisfies
\begin{align*}
k=6&:\quad n\le50,\\
k=7&:\quad n=50\text{ in diameter two, or }n\le74\text{ in diameter three},\\
k=8&:\quad n\le108,\\
k=9&:\quad n\le150.
\end{align*}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The degree exclusion is Theorem~\ref{thm:regular-degree-six},
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-diameter} excludes diameter at least five, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:diameter-four} removes diameter four for \(k\le9\).
 For diameter three, Theorem~\ref{thm:three-to-one} gives, for
\(k=6,7,8,9\), respectively,
\[
 n\le50,\ 75,\ 108,\ 150.
\]
Since \(7n=2|E(G)|\), the degree-seven bound improves to \(74\).  The
diameter-two alternatives are determined by the Moore multiplicities: only
the degree-seven, order-\(50\) Hoffman--Singleton case survives.
\end{proof}

Appendix~\ref{app:three-to-one-arithmetic} lists the corresponding order bounds
through degree twenty, including the parity adjustment for odd degrees.

The degree-six order bound leaves the endpoint \(n=50\) unresolved.
Appendix~\ref{app:order50-details} gives further restrictions on adjacency
multiplicity and cycle counts, and studies a signed graph associated with
the excess matrix. These are necessary conditions; they do not establish
whether a strict counterexample at this endpoint exists.

\section{Distance spectra after vertex deletion in Moore graphs}\label{sec:punctures}

Deleting vertices can increase the distances between those that remain.
We identify these increases and use them to bound the change in the least
distance eigenvalue. The full one- and two-deletion spectra are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:small-deletion-spectra}.

Let \(M\) be a degree-\(k\) Moore graph of diameter two and put
\(\Delta=\sqrt{4k-3}\).

\subsection{Changed distances and a stability bound}

\begin{theorem}[Small-puncture normal form]\label{thm:small-puncture}
Let \(M\) be a degree-\(k\) Moore graph of diameter two, let
\(S\subseteq V(M)\) have size \(s\le k-1\), and put \(H=M-S\).  Then \(H\)
is connected, has diameter at most three, and
\[
 \resultbox{\delta^*(H)=k-\frac{s}{k}.}
\]
Let \(B\) be the surviving-vertex by deleted-vertex incidence matrix, with
\(B_{xz}=1\) exactly when \(x\sim_M z\).  Then
\[
 \resultbox{
 D(H)=2(J-I)-A(H)+BB^{\mathsf T}
 -\operatorname{diag}(BB^{\mathsf T}).
 }
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The case \(s=0\) is immediate, so assume \(1\le s\le k-1\).  Let
\(x,y\in V(H)\) be nonadjacent in \(M\), and suppose their unique common
neighbour \(z\) lies in \(S\).  For every
\(a\in N_M(x)\setminus\{z\}\), the vertices \(a,y\) are nonadjacent and
have a unique common neighbour \(b_a\).  Thus
\[
 x-a-b_a-y
\]
is a length-three path.  The \(k-1\) paths are internally vertex-disjoint:
if \(b_a=b_{a'}\), there would be a four-cycle, and if \(b_a=a'\),
there would be a triangle through \(x\).  Apart from \(z\), only
\(s-1\le k-2\) vertices have been deleted, so they cannot meet all these
paths.  At least one survives.  Thus every destroyed length-two path is
replaced by distance exactly three, giving the matrix formula.

For \(x\in V(H)\), let \(t_x=|N_M(x)\cap S|\).  A deleted neighbour of
\(x\) is adjacent to no surviving neighbour of \(x\), by triangle-freeness;
each other deleted vertex has at most one common neighbour with \(x\).  Thus
\[
 \sum_{y\in N_H(x)}t_y\le s-t_x.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 d_H^*(x)
 \ge k-\frac{s-t_x}{k-t_x}
 \ge k-\frac{s}{k},
\]
where the last inequality follows from
\[
 \left(k-\frac{s-t}{k-t}\right)-\left(k-\frac{s}{k}\right)
 =\frac{t(k-s)}{k(k-t)}\ge0.
\]
The intersection bound
\[
 \left|\bigcap_{z\in S}\Gamma_2(z)\right|
 \ge k^2+1-s(k+1)\ge2
\]
provides a surviving vertex \(x\) at distance two from every deleted
vertex.  This vertex attains the lower bound.  For \(z\in S\), let
\(y_z\) be the unique common neighbour of \(x,z\).  It cannot be deleted:
\(y_z\in S\) would, by the choice of \(x\), require \(d_M(x,y_z)=2\),
contrary to \(x\sim y_z\).  All these common neighbours therefore survive, and
each deleted vertex contributes once to the neighbour-degree deficit.
The lower bound is consequently an equality.  The
replacement paths, boundary case \(s=k-1\), distance formula, and attainment
step are independently checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_12_small_puncture.py}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Deletion stability]\label{prop:deletion-stability}
Let \(G\) be connected with at least two vertices, let
\(S\subseteq V(G)\), and suppose \(H=G-S\) is connected with at least two
vertices.  Put
\[
 D_0=D(G)[V(H)],
 \qquad E_S=D(H)-D_0,
\]
\[
 a=\delta^*(G),
 \quad b=\delta^*(H),
 \quad \gamma=\Phi(G).
\]
Then
\[
 \resultbox{
 \Phi(H)\ge\gamma-(a-b)+\lambda_{\min}(E_S).
 }
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The score \(\Phi(H)\) is the least eigenvalue of \(D(H)+bI\).
We write this matrix as
\[
 D(H)+bI=(D_0+aI)+E_S-(a-b)I.
\]
The first term is a principal submatrix of \(D(G)+aI\), whose least
eigenvalue is \(\gamma\).  Principal-submatrix interlacing
\cite[Corollary~2.2]{Haemers1995} therefore gives
\(\lambda_{\min}(D_0+aI)\ge\gamma\).
Weyl's inequality bounds the least eigenvalue after adding \(E_S\),
and subtracting \((a-b)I\) shifts every eigenvalue by \(-(a-b)\).
This gives the stated bound.  For Moore punctures, \(E_S\) is the
explicit distance-increase matrix specified in Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Uniform deletion stability]
\label{cor:uniform-deletion}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}, put
\[
 t_x=|N_M(x)\cap S|,
 \qquad
 \tau(S)=\max_{x\in V(M-S)}t_x.
\]
Then
\[
 \resultbox{
 \Phi(M-S)\ge
 k-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2-\frac{s}{k}-\tau(S).
 }
\]
In particular, the deletion is a strict counterexample whenever the
right-hand side is positive.  Since \(\tau(S)\le s\), every deletion of
\(s\) vertices is strict whenever
\[
 \resultbox{
 s\left(1+\frac1k\right)
 <k-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2.
 }
\]
For \(k>3\), equivalently, every deletion of at most
\[
 r_k=\min\left\{
 k-1,\
 \left\lceil
 \frac{k}{k+1}\left(k-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2\right)
 \right\rceil-1
 \right\}
\]
vertices is strict.  In particular, \(r_7=2\); for a hypothetical
degree-\(57\) Moore graph, whose existence remains open
\cite{SmithMontemanni2026}, this estimate gives \(r_{57}=47\).
Proposition~\ref{prop:clique-deletion-bound} strengthens that
guarantee to every deletion of at most \(56\) vertices.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}, the distance-increase matrix is
\[
 E_S=BB^{\mathsf T}-\operatorname{diag}(t_x).
\]
Since \(BB^{\mathsf T}\succeq0\) and
\(\operatorname{diag}(t_x)\preceq\tau(S)I\), one has
\(\lambda_{\min}(E_S)\ge-\tau(S)\).  The parent Moore graph has score
\[
 \Phi(M)=k-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2,
\]
and Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture} shows that deletion lowers the minimum
dual degree by \(s/k\).  Proposition~\ref{prop:deletion-stability} now gives
the first bound.  The second follows from \(\tau(S)\le s\), and solving its
strict scalar inequality for the largest integral \(s\) gives \(r_k\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{One and two vertex deletions}

For one deleted vertex, and for the endpoints of an edge when \(k\ge3\),
the least distance eigenvalue is \(-2-\sqrt{k}\). Combining this with the
dual-degree formula gives, respectively,
\[
 \Phi(M-v)=k-\frac1k-2-\sqrt{k},
 \qquad
 \Phi(M-\{u,v\})=k-\frac2k-2-\sqrt{k}\quad(uv\in E(M)).
\]
Both scores are positive for every realizable integer \(k\ge5\).
Theorems~\ref{thm:one-puncture} and~\ref{thm:edge-puncture} give the
complete spectra in Appendix~\ref{app:small-deletion-spectra}.
For two nonadjacent deletions, Theorem~\ref{thm:nonadjacent-puncture}
also gives a full factorization and proves strictness for every realizable
integer \(k\ge6\). The stability bound above avoids these diagonalizations
when only strictness is needed.

\section{Uniform deletion bounds and the Hoffman--Singleton radius}
\label{sec:robustness}

The stability bound from Section~\ref{sec:punctures} handles many deletion
sets without a new spectral calculation. We now improve it when the deleted
vertices share a surviving neighbour. This proves stability through three
deletions for Hoffman--Singleton. Exact orbit certificates cover sizes four
and five and determine the sharp universal radius.

For a common surviving neighbour, the distance correction is a union of
cliques. Its least eigenvalue gives the sharper bound below.

\begin{proposition}[Neighbourhood punctures]
\label{prop:neighbourhood-puncture}
Let \(M\) be a degree-\(k\) Moore graph of diameter two, \(k\ge2\).
Let \(w\in V(M)\), let \(\varnothing\ne S\subseteq N_M(w)\), and put
\(s=|S|\le k-1\) and \(H=M-S\).  The distance-increase matrix
\[
 E_S=D(H)-D(M)[V(H)]
\]
is the adjacency matrix of \(s\) copies of \(K_k\) meeting only at \(w\),
together with \(k(k-s)\) isolated vertices.  In particular,
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(E_S)
 =\frac{k-2-\sqrt{(k-2)^2+4s(k-1)}}2,
\]
and
\[
 \Phi(H)\ge
 k-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2-\frac{s}{k}
 +\frac{k-2-\sqrt{(k-2)^2+4s(k-1)}}2.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The deleted set is independent, since a pair of its vertices joined by an
edge would form a triangle with \(w\).  Thus each deleted vertex has all
\(k\) of its neighbours in \(H\).  For distinct \(z,z'\in S\), the Moore
property gives
\[
 N_M(z)\cap N_M(z')=\{w\}.
\]
By Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}, every destroyed length-two path is
replaced by a length-three path.  Hence \((E_S)_{xy}=1\), for distinct
surviving vertices \(x,y\), precisely when they belong to \(N_M(z)\) for
some \(z\in S\).  There is at most one such \(z\).  These neighbourhoods
therefore give the asserted cliques, with \(k(k-s)\) remaining vertices.

We take the coordinate vector at the centre and the indicator vector of
all noncentral clique vertices.  Their span is invariant under the
correction matrix.  After normalizing both vectors to have length one,
the matrix on this span is the normalized symmetric quotient
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}
 0&\sqrt{s(k-1)}\\
 \sqrt{s(k-1)}&k-2
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The other eigenvalues are \(-1\) on the zero-sum space of the \(k-1\)
noncentral vertices of each clique, \(k-2\) on the differences of their
constant sums, and \(0\) on the isolated vertices.  These spaces have
dimensions \(s(k-2)\), \(s-1\), and \(k(k-s)\), respectively, so the
decomposition is complete.  The smaller quotient root is at most \(-1\), because its
characteristic polynomial has value \((k-1)(1-s)\le0\) at \(-1\).
It is therefore the least eigenvalue of \(E_S\).
The final inequality follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:deletion-stability}
and \(\delta^*(H)=k-s/k\).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Analytic three-vertex stability]
\label{cor:analytic-three-puncture}
Let \(M\) be the Hoffman--Singleton graph.  For every
\(S\subseteq V(M)\) with \(|S|\le3\),
\[
 \Phi(M-S)>\frac17.
\]
In particular, every such puncture is a strict counterexample to WOW-284.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The graph \(M\) has score \(3\).  If \(|S|\le2\),
Corollary~\ref{cor:uniform-deletion} gives
\(\Phi(M-S)\ge3-|S|/7-|S|\ge5/7\).
Now let \(|S|=3\), and put
\[
 \tau=\max_{x\in V(M-S)}|N_M(x)\cap S|.
\]
If \(\tau\le2\), the same corollary gives
\(\Phi(M-S)\ge3-3/7-2=4/7\).
If \(\tau=3\), some surviving vertex \(w\) is adjacent to all three
deleted vertices.  Proposition~\ref{prop:neighbourhood-puncture} gives
\[
 \Phi(M-S)\ge\frac{71-7\sqrt{97}}{14}>\frac17,
\]
where the strict comparison follows from
\(49\cdot97=4753<4761=69^2\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Hoffman--Singleton robustness radius]\label{thm:hs-radius}
Let \(M\) be the Hoffman--Singleton graph.  Every induced graph \(M-S\) with
\(|S|\le5\) is a strict counterexample to WOW-284.  This is sharp in the
universal sense: there exists a six-vertex set whose deletion is not strict.
Hence the universal vertex-deletion robustness radius is exactly five.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture} gives
\[
 \delta^*(M-S)=\frac{49-|S|}{7}.
\]
Corollary~\ref{cor:analytic-three-puncture} proves strictness analytically
for every set with \(|S|\le3\).  Only sets of sizes four and five remain
to be certified.  We reduce that finite check by automorphisms.  Two
explicitly stored permutations are verified edge by edge on the coordinate
graph, and they generate a group of order \(252000\).  The full orbit
inventory on deletion sets of sizes \(0,1,2,3,4,5\) has counts
\[
 1,1,2,4,11,33.
\]
Thus the remaining sizes require \(44\) representatives.  For each one,
exact fraction arithmetic gives
\[
 7D(M-S)+(49-|S|)I\succ0.
\]
The archived certificates for all \(52\) original representatives are
retained; those for the smaller sets provide redundant checks.

To show that the radius cannot be increased, delete
\[
 \{P_{2,4},P_{3,1},P_{3,4},Q_{2,1},Q_{3,4},Q_{4,4}\}.
\]
The resulting graph has \(\delta^*=43/7\), while an exact
\(LDL^{\mathsf T}\) decomposition of \(7D(M-S)+43I\) has exactly one negative
and no zero pivot.  Since \(L\) is invertible, Sylvester's law of inertia makes
\(7D(M-S)+43I\) indefinite, so the graph is not strict.  Generator action,
orbit exhaustion, BFS distances, the small-puncture formula, and a handwritten rational
\(LDL^{\mathsf T}\) implementation are independently checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_13_hs_robustness.py}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The theorem asserts that every deletion through size five succeeds and that at
least one deletion of size six fails.  It does not assert that every
six-vertex deletion fails.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Six-neighbour punctures]
\label{prop:hs-neighbourhood-six}
Let \(M\) be the Hoffman--Singleton graph.  For every \(w\in V(M)\) and
every \(S\subseteq N_M(w)\) with \(|S|=6\), the graph \(M-S\) is a
strict counterexample to WOW-284.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put \(H=M-S\). Each vertex in \(\Gamma_2(w)\) has a unique
neighbour in \(N_M(w)\), which we call its parent; the vertices adjacent to
a given parent in the second sphere are its children.  Partition \(V(H)\) into \(\{w\},A,B,C\), where
\(A=N_M(w)\setminus S\), \(B\) consists of children of the deleted
parents, and \(C\) consists of children of the surviving parents.  Their
sizes are
\[
 1,\qquad1,\qquad36,\qquad6.
\]
The Moore property gives no edge between children of the same parent and
exactly one neighbour in every other child class.  Indeed, if \(x\) has
parent \(p\) and \(q\in N_M(w)\setminus\{p\}\), the unique common
neighbour of the nonadjacent pair \(x,q\) belongs to \(\Gamma_2(w)\)
and has parent \(q\).

The distance sum from a vertex to each part depends only on its own part.
For example, a vertex of \(B\) has five siblings at distance three, five
other vertices of \(B\) at distance one, and twenty-five at distance two.
Its distance sum to \(B\) is therefore \(5\cdot3+5\cdot1+25\cdot2=70\).
Thus the partition is equitable for the
distance matrix. On vectors constant on each part, the distance operator
acts by the following row-sum quotient, in the order \(\{w\},A,B,C\):
\[
 Q=\begin{pmatrix}
 0&1&108&12\\
 1&0&72&6\\
 3&2&70&11\\
 2&1&66&10
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The distances in the original Moore graph, restricted to the surviving vertices, are given by
\(D_0=2(J-I)-A(H)\).  Proposition~\ref{prop:neighbourhood-puncture}
changes only the distances from \(w\) to \(B\) and between two children
of the same deleted parent, adding one in each case.

Let \(U\) be the four-dimensional space of vectors constant on each part.
Its orthogonal complement \(U^\perp\) consists of vectors whose coordinates
sum to zero separately on each part. Both \(D_0\) and the distance-increase
matrix \(E_S\) preserve \(U\). Because they are symmetric, they also
preserve \(U^\perp\), so the two spaces can be checked separately.

We first bound the correction on \(U^\perp\). The \(w\)-coordinate is zero
because its part has only one vertex. For \(x\in U^\perp\),
\[
 x^{\mathsf T}E_Sx
 =\sum_{z\in S}\left(\sum_{y\in N_M(z)\setminus\{w\}}x_y\right)^2
   -\sum_{y\in B}x_y^2
 \ge-\lVert x\rVert^2.
\]
The distance matrix of the original Moore graph has least eigenvalue \(-4\).
Taking its principal submatrix on the surviving vertices gives
\(D_0\succeq-4I\), and the correction estimate above gives
\(D(H)|_{U^\perp}\succeq-5I\).
Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture} gives
\(\delta^*(H)=43/7>5\).

It remains to check the restriction to \(U\).  In the basis of cell
indicators, the quadratic form of \(7D(H)+43I\) has symmetric matrix
\[
 T=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,36,6)(7Q+43I)
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 43&7&756&84\\
 7&43&504&42\\
 756&504&19188&2772\\
 84&42&2772&678
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its leading principal minors are
\[
 43,\qquad1800,\qquad4374000,\qquad226834992.
\]
All four are positive, so Sylvester's criterion gives
\(T\succ0\).  The shifted distance matrix is therefore positive
definite on both \(U\) and \(U^\perp\), proving \(\Phi(H)>0\).
\end{proof}

These successful six-vertex deletions do not increase the universal radius:
the deletion in Theorem~\ref{thm:hs-radius} still fails.

For a hypothetical degree-\(57\) Moore graph, the neighbourhood
calculation reaches the endpoint of the small-puncture theorem.

\begin{corollary}[Degree-\(57\) neighbourhood punctures]
\label{cor:degree57-neighbourhood}
If \(M\) is a degree-\(57\) Moore graph of diameter two, then for every
\(w\in V(M)\) and every \(S\subseteq N_M(w)\) with \(|S|\le56\),
\[
 \Phi(M-S)>\frac{685}{57}.
\]
In particular, every such induced graph is a strict counterexample to
WOW-284.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:moore-threshold} gives the original Moore graph a score of \(48\), which
handles \(S=\varnothing\).  For nonempty \(S\), put \(s=|S|\).
Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture} applies through \(s=56\): the survivor
graph is connected and has minimum dual degree \(57-s/57\).  Its order
is at least \(3194\), and it inherits girth at least five.
Proposition~\ref{prop:neighbourhood-puncture} gives
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(E_S)
 =\frac{55-\sqrt{55^2+224s}}2>-35,
\]
since
\[
 55^2+224s\le15569<15625=125^2.
\]
The loss of minimum dual degree is exactly \(s/57\), so
Proposition~\ref{prop:deletion-stability} yields
\[
 \Phi(M-S)\ge48-\frac{s}{57}+\lambda_{\min}(E_S)
 >48-\frac{56}{57}-35=\frac{685}{57}>0.
\]
\end{proof}

Grouping the correction cliques through one surviving vertex improves
the estimate for arbitrary deletion sets.

\begin{proposition}[Splitting the distance correction at a vertex]
\label{prop:clique-deletion-bound}
Let \(M\) be a degree-\(k\) Moore graph of diameter two, \(k\ge2\),
and let \(S\subseteq V(M)\) have size \(s\le k-1\).  Put \(H=M-S\),
\[
 E_S=D(H)-D(M)[V(H)],
 \qquad
 \tau=\max_{x\in V(H)}|N_M(x)\cap S|,
\]
and, for \(0\le r\le k-1\), define
\[
 f_k(r)=\frac{k-2-\sqrt{(k-2)^2+4r(k-1)}}2.
\]
Then
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(E_S)
 \ge \max\{-\tau,\,f_k(\tau)-s+\tau\},
\]
and
\[
 \Phi(H)\ge k-\frac{3+\sqrt{4k-3}}2-\frac{s}{k}
 +\max\{-\tau,\,f_k(\tau)-s+\tau\}.
\]
In particular, if \(M\) has degree \(57\), then every
\(S\subseteq V(M)\) with \(|S|\le56\) satisfies
\[
 \Phi(M-S)\ge\frac{286}{57}>0.
\]
Thus every such puncture is a strict counterexample to WOW-284.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For \(s=0\), one has \(\tau=0\), \(f_k(0)=0\), and \(E_S=0\), so the
claims follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:moore-threshold}.  Assume \(s\ge1\).
For \(z\in S\), let \(b_z\) be the indicator vector of
\(N_M(z)\cap V(H)\), and put
\[
 C_z=b_zb_z^{\mathsf T}-\operatorname{diag}(b_z).
\]
The matrix \(C_z\) records the distance increases caused by deleting
\(z\): it is the adjacency matrix of a clique on the surviving neighbours
of \(z\), with all other vertices isolated.
Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture} gives \(E_S=\sum_{z\in S}C_z\).
Each clique matrix has least eigenvalue at least \(-1\), since
\[
 C_z+I=b_zb_z^{\mathsf T}
       +\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf1-b_z)\succeq0.
\]
The Gram-matrix estimate also gives \(E_S\succeq-\tau I\).

To obtain the second bound, choose \(w\in V(H)\) attaining \(\tau\),
and put \(Z=N_M(w)\cap S\).  Every deleted vertex has a surviving
neighbour, since \(s<k\), so \(1\le|Z|=\tau\le k-1\).
We group the \(\tau\) clique matrices indexed by \(Z\) and estimate
the remaining \(s-\tau\) separately.
The correction matrix for deleting \(Z\) alone is
\[
 F_Z=D(M-Z)-D(M)[V(M-Z)].
\]
By the small-puncture formula, its principal submatrix on \(V(H)\)
is exactly \(\sum_{z\in Z}C_z\).
Proposition~\ref{prop:neighbourhood-puncture} gives
\(\lambda_{\min}(F_Z)=f_k(\tau)\), so interlacing bounds the least
eigenvalue of this grouped sum below by \(f_k(\tau)\).
Each of the remaining clique matrices has least eigenvalue at least
\(-1\).  Weyl's inequality therefore yields
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(E_S)
 \ge f_k(\tau)-(s-\tau).
\]
Together with \(E_S\succeq-\tau I\), this proves the first inequality.
The score bound follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:deletion-stability}
and \(\delta^*(H)=k-s/k\).

For \(k=57\), the original Moore graph has score \(48\).  If \(\tau\le42\), the first
bound gives \(\lambda_{\min}(E_S)\ge-42\).  If \(\tau\ge43\), then
\[
 (27+2\tau)^2-(55^2+224\tau)
 =4\bigl((\tau-43)(\tau+14)+28\bigr)>0.
\]
Since \(27+2\tau>0\), this implies
\(f_{57}(\tau)>14-\tau\), and hence
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(E_S)>14-s\ge-42.
\]
Consequently, for every \(s\le56\),
\[
 \Phi(M-S)\ge48-\frac{s}{57}-42
 \ge48-\frac{56}{57}-42=\frac{286}{57}>0.
\]
\end{proof}

\section{The equality boundary and a graph attaining it}
\label{sec:controls}

The shifted adjacency window also identifies the equality case of WOW-284.
We state the criterion and give an exact graph attaining it.

\begin{theorem}[Equality boundary]\label{thm:equality-boundary}
Let \(G\) be connected, \(k\)-regular, of girth at least five and diameter
three.  Then
\[
 \Phi(G)=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \max_{\theta\ne k}|\theta+1|=\sqrt{2k-2}.
\]
Equivalently, \(D+kI\) is positive semidefinite and singular. When \(\Phi(G)=0\) and \(2k-2\) is
not a square, the two boundary adjacency eigenvalues occur with equal
multiplicity, so the distance eigenvalue \(-k\) has even multiplicity.  If
\(2k-2\) is a square, then \(k=2r^2+1\) for some integer \(r\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This is immediate from Theorem~\ref{thm:diameter-three-score}.  In the
nonsquare case, the two boundary values are algebraic conjugates, so their
multiplicities in the integral characteristic polynomial agree.  In the square
case, \(2k-2=s^2\) forces \(s\) even.  The exact scalar audit is
\codefile{scripts/verify_equality_boundary.py}.
\end{proof}

J{\o}rgensen's \(9\)-regular order-\(96\) graph of girth five is an exact equality
case:
\[
 \delta^*=9,
 \qquad \lambda_{\min}(D)=-9,
\]
with multiplicity eight.  The construction is due to J{\o}rgensen
\cite{Jorgensen2005}; the three local graph representations, handwritten
graph6 decoder, characteristic polynomials, root intervals, and provenance
boundary are checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_09_jorgensen96.py}.

Appendix~\ref{app:excluded-constructions} defines a balanced prime-field
family of girth-five graphs and rules out strict counterexamples in that
family when the diameter is three. It also rules out every layer-respecting
perfect-matching deletion of Hoffman--Singleton considered there.  These calculations test the
window criterion but are not needed for the counterexamples or the deletion
bounds above.

\section{Open questions on spectral constraints and stability}\label{sec:scope}

The necessary conditions at degree six and order $50$ do not settle
existence. It remains to determine whether a graph satisfying them is a
strict counterexample. The present results also do not determine the minimum
order of a strict counterexample. Extending the regular-graph order bounds
to irregular graphs is another direction.

\subsection{The adjacency multiplicity bound at order fifty}

\begin{proposition}[The order-\(50\) \((-2)\)-multiplicity bound]
\label{prop:order50-minus-two}
Let \(G\) be \(6\)-regular, of order \(50\), and of girth at least five.
The multiplicity \(m_{-2}(A)\) of the adjacency eigenvalue \(-2\) satisfies
\[
 m_{-2}(A)\le20.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We isolate the eigenvalue whose multiplicity is to be bounded.  Put
\(m=m_{-2}(A)\), remove the principal eigenvalue \(6\) and the \(m\)
copies of \(-2\), and write \(\mu_0,\ldots,\mu_4\) for the moments of
the remaining eigenvalues.  Girth at least five gives
\[
 \tr A=0,\quad \tr A^2=300,\quad \tr A^3=0,\quad \tr A^4=3300,
\]
and therefore
\[
 (\mu_0,\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3,\mu_4)
 =(49-m,-6+2m,264-4m,-216+8m,2004-16m).
\]
The moment matrix
\[
 H=\begin{pmatrix}
 \mu_0&\mu_1&\mu_2\\
 \mu_1&\mu_2&\mu_3\\
 \mu_2&\mu_3&\mu_4
 \end{pmatrix}
\]
is the Gram matrix obtained by evaluating the constant, linear, and
quadratic monomials at the remaining eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity.
It is therefore positive semidefinite, so its determinant is nonnegative.
Expanding that determinant gives
\[
 \det H=3600(1625-81m)\ge0.
\]
Since \(m\) is an integer, \(m\le20\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cycle counts and signed structure at order fifty}

The order bound still allows the degree-six boundary case.  We now
restrict its possible cycle counts and signed Gram representation.  These
are necessary conditions; they do not settle existence.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:order50-feasibility}
Let \(G\) be a connected \(6\)-regular graph of order \(50\) and girth
at least five, and suppose \(\Phi(G)>0\).  Then \(G\) has diameter three.
For an edge \(e\), let \(\sigma_e\) be the number of five-cycles containing
\(e\).  Then \(\sigma_e\in\{12,13\}\).  Call \(e\) low or high according as
\(\sigma_e=12\) or \(13\), respectively, and let \(H\) be the spanning
subgraph of high edges.  Put \(m=|E(H)|\), and let \(\tau(v)\) be the number
of five-cycles through \(v\).  Then
\[
 \tau(v)\in\{36,37,38\},
 \qquad d_H(v)=2\tau(v)-72\in\{0,2,4\},
\]
\[
 m\equiv0\pmod5,
 \qquad N_5=360+\frac m5.
\]
For a two-edge path \(u-v-w\), put
\[
 R_{uvw}=6\alpha_{uvw}+\beta_{uvw},
\]
where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) count the five- and six-cycles containing the
path.  The allowed values are
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{types of the two incident edges}&R_{uvw}\\
\hline
\text{low--low}&30,31,32\\
\text{mixed}&30,31,32\\
\text{high--high}&30,31.
\end{array}
\]
Writing \(S_2=\sum_vd_H(v)^2\), one has
\[
 1950-m\le N_6
 \le2200-\frac{5m}{6}-\frac{S_2}{12},
\]
\[
 \frac{43m^2-70200m+119632500}{58500}
 \le N_6
 \le\frac{4220000-2200m-7m^2}{2000}.
\]
Writing
\[
 n_i=\bigl|\{v\in V(G):d_H(v)=i\}\bigr|
 \qquad(i\in\{0,2,4\}),
\]
exact enumeration leaves \(266\) triples \((n_0,n_2,n_4)\) such that
\[
 n_0+n_2+n_4=50,\qquad
 m=n_2+2n_4\equiv0\pmod5,
\]
and at least one integer \(N_6\) satisfies all four displayed bounds.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The diameter reduction used in Theorem~\ref{thm:degree-six-fifty} applies
verbatim: diameter at least four gives a Rayleigh quotient at most \(-8\), and
diameter two gives the trace contradiction from
\((x-6)(x^2+x-5)^{18}\).  Hence the diameter is three.
Proposition~\ref{prop:edge-cycle} gives
\[
 L_{6,50}=12,\qquad U_{6,50}=\frac{346}{25}<14.
\]
Since \(\sigma_e\) is integral, \(\sigma_e\in\{12,13\}\) for every edge
\(e\).  Around a fixed vertex \(v\), write
\(\Gamma_i=\Gamma_i(v)\) for the distance layers; their sizes are
\(1,6,30,13\).  We use the four distance layers to bound the five-cycle count.
If \(\tau\) is the number of five-cycles through \(v\), take one
indicator vector for each layer and normalize it to have length one.
The adjacency quadratic form on their span has a symmetric matrix,
the layer compression.  This matrix is similar to the average row
quotient.  Its characteristic polynomial is \((x-6)q_\tau(x)\), where
\[
 195q_\tau(x)
 =195x^3+(2250-43\tau)x^2+(105-30\tau)x+228\tau-11250.
\]
In particular,
\[
 195q_\tau(-1+\sqrt{10})
 =(-215+56\sqrt{10})\tau+7350-1860\sqrt{10}.
\]
The coefficient of \(\tau\) is negative because
\(56^2\cdot10<215^2\), and at \(\tau=39\) the right-hand side is
\(9(-115+36\sqrt{10})<0\).  Also
\[
 195q_\tau(6)=1500(75-\tau).
\]
Every vertex of \(\Gamma_2\) has exactly one neighbour in \(\Gamma_1\), and
the edges inside \(\Gamma_2\) are in bijection with the five-cycles through
\(v\).  Hence
\[
 e(\Gamma_2,\Gamma_3)
 =30\cdot5-2e(\Gamma_2)
 =150-2\tau>0,
\]
because every vertex of the nonempty layer \(\Gamma_3\) has a neighbour in
\(\Gamma_2\).  Thus \(q_\tau(6)>0\), whereas for \(\tau\ge39\) its value
at the upper end of the WOW window is negative.  We obtain a root in
\((-1+\sqrt{10},6)\), corresponding to a nonprincipal eigenvalue of the
layer compression.  By interlacing, the adjacency matrix then has a
nonprincipal eigenvalue above the window, which is impossible.
Conversely,
\[
 150-2\tau=e(\Gamma_2,\Gamma_3)\le13\cdot6,
\]
so \(\tau\ge36\).  Hence \(\tau\in\{36,37,38\}\).  Finally,
\(\sum_{e\ni v}\sigma_e=2\tau(v)\), so the high-edge degree is
\(2\tau(v)-72\); counting edge--five-cycle incidences gives
\(5N_5=12\cdot150+m\).

The next restrictions come from a two-path \(u-v-w\). Girth at least
five gives \((A^3)_{uw}=\alpha_{uvw}\) and
\((A^4)_{uw}=16+\beta_{uvw}\). Let \(h_e=0\) for a low edge and
\(h_e=1\) for a high edge. Substitution into the centered slack matrix gives
\[
 25\mathcal S_6[\{u,v,w\}]=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 48&-2-25h_{uv}&773-25R_{uvw}\\
 -2-25h_{uv}&48&-2-25h_{vw}\\
 773-25R_{uvw}&-2-25h_{vw}&48
 \end{pmatrix}\succeq0.
\]
The \(2\times2\) minors give \(R_{uvw}\in\{29,30,31,32\}\).
At \(R_{uvw}=29\), the Gram norm of \(e_u-e_w\) vanishes, so this
vector lies in the kernel of \(\mathcal S_6\). On \(\one^\perp\),
\(\mathcal S_6=-g_6(A)\). The strict shifted window excludes the
endpoint zeros of \(g_6\), leaving only its double zero at \(-2\).
Thus \(A(e_u-e_w)=-2(e_u-e_w)\), which is impossible: its
\(u\)-coordinate would have to be both zero and \(-2\).
If both edges are high and \(R_{uvw}=32\), the displayed determinant
is \(-33750\), also impossible. This proves the local table.

We now make the cycle count explicit. Sum over unoriented two-edge paths.
Each five-cycle contains five such paths and each six-cycle contains six,
so
\[
 \sum_{u-v-w}R_{uvw}=30N_5+6N_6
                   =10800+6m+6N_6.
\]
There are \(50\binom62=750\) paths, of which
\[
 \sum_v\binom{d_H(v)}2=\frac{S_2-2m}{2}
\]
have two high edges. Every local value is at least \(30\) and at most
\(32\), with upper bound \(31\) for a high--high path. Hence
\[
 22500\le10800+6m+6N_6
 \le24000-\frac{S_2-2m}{2}.
\]
Rearranging gives the first pair of \(N_6\) bounds.
For the second pair, we evaluate polynomials on the shifted nonprincipal
spectrum. A Gram matrix gives the lower bound, and a weighted Gram matrix
gives the upper bound; the spectral window makes the weights positive.
Appendix~\ref{app:order50-moment-bounds} displays both matrices and their
determinant calculations.
The exact matrix calculations and enumeration are checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_06_order50_feasibility.py}.
The $266$ surviving profiles neither establish nor rule out a graph.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Signed-root Gram formulation]\label{rem:signed-root}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:order50-feasibility}, put
\[
 T=50J-g_6(A)-2I.
\]
Then
\[
 T\one=2\one,\qquad T+2I\succeq0,
 \qquad 25\mathcal S_6=25(T+2I)-2J.
\]
This is the specialization of Proposition~\ref{prop:signed-complement}:
the excess parameter is \(r=42\), so the signed row sum is \(2\).
For vertices \(u,z\) at distance three, put
\[
 q_{uz}=6(A^3)_{uz}+(A^4)_{uz}.
\]
The \(2\times2\) estimate gives \(q_{uz}\in\{48,49,50,51\}\), and the kernel
argument in Theorem~\ref{thm:integral-slack} excludes \(48\).  Together with
the edge and two-path intervals in Theorem~\ref{thm:order50-feasibility}, this
gives
\[
 T_{uv}\in\{-1,0,1\}\qquad(u\ne v),\qquad T_{uu}=0.
\]
 Consequently \(T+2I\) is the Gram matrix of \(50\) vectors of norm
 \(\sqrt2\), with inner products in \(\{-1,0,1\}\) between distinct vectors.
 Its row sum is \(4\); the signed adjacency matrix \(T\) has row sum \(2\).  Its kernel is \(E_{-2}(A)\), so
 Proposition~\ref{prop:order50-minus-two} gives
 \[
  \operatorname{rank}(T+2I)\ge30.
 \]
Thus the unresolved degree-six boundary can be viewed as a
 root-type integral Gram problem constrained by the graph's distance classes
 and local cycle data.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why the signed complement is disconnected}

\begin{theorem}[Disconnected signed complement]\label{thm:order50-disconnected}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:order50-feasibility}, the edge-signed
graph with adjacency matrix \(T\) from Remark~\ref{rem:signed-root} is
disconnected.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(N_i\) denote the number of \(i\)-cycles in \(G\).  In terms of the degree-six
nonbacktracking polynomials,
\[
\begin{aligned}
(g_6+2)^2={}&28144F_0+18220F_1+8838F_2+3576F_3+1233F_4\\
&+352F_5+78F_6+12F_7+F_8.
\end{aligned}
\]
Girth at least five gives \(\tr F_i(A)=0\) for \(1\le i\le4\).  For
lengths five and six, every closed nonbacktracking walk is a directed cycle.
At lengths seven and eight there are also the walks obtained by attaching a
one-edge tail to a directed five- or six-cycle.  Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
\tr F_5(A)&=10N_5,&
\tr F_6(A)&=12N_6,\\
\tr F_7(A)&=14N_7+40N_5,&
\tr F_8(A)&=16N_8+48N_6.
\end{aligned}
\]
These walk counts give the full trace of the squared polynomial.  On the
nonprincipal space, the signed matrix and the polynomial differ only by a
sign, so their squares have the same trace there.  We therefore subtract
the principal adjacency contribution and add the square of the signed
matrix's eigenvalue \(2\) on the constant vector, giving
\[
\tr T^2
=8(500N_5+123N_6+21N_7+2N_8-604100).
\]
If \(P_+\) and \(P_-\) are the numbers of positive and negative signed edges,
then the signed row sum and the zero diagonal give
\[
P_+-P_-=50,\qquad
\tr T^2=2(P_++P_-)=100+4P_-.
\]
The polynomial expression for the trace is a multiple of eight.
Comparing it with the signed-edge count shows that \(P_-\) is odd.

We show that connectedness is incompatible with this parity.  Suppose
\(T\) is connected.  Proposition~\ref{prop:order50-minus-two} gives
\(\operatorname{rank}(T+2I)\ge30\).  The representation theorem for
connected edge-signed graphs with smallest eigenvalue at least \(-2\)
\cite[Theorem~2]{GreavesEtAl2015} represents \(T+2I\) in a root system of
type \(D_\ell\) or \(E_8\).  The rank bound rules out \(E_8\), leaving
\[
B^{\mathsf T}B=T+2I
\]
for an integral matrix \(B\) whose columns \(b_u\) are roots
\(\pm e_i\pm e_j\).  Put \(s=B\one\).  Since
\((T+2I)\one=4\one\),
\[
b_u\mathbin{\cdot}s=4\quad\text{for every }u,
\qquad
\lVert s\rVert^2=200.
\]
Changing signs of coordinate axes if necessary, assume \(s_i\ge0\).
Form a multigraph with the used coordinates as vertices and one edge
for each root column, joining its two nonzero coordinates.
This is the coordinate-support multigraph.
It is connected: otherwise the columns of \(B\) would split into
two families with zero inner products between them, and \(T\)
would be disconnected.  If \(v\) coordinates are used, then
\[
30\le v\le51:
\]
the lower bound is the Gram rank, and a connected support multigraph with
fifty root-edges has at most fifty-one vertices.

Every root joins two coordinate levels satisfying
\(\pm s_i\pm s_j=4\).  Along such an edge, the levels either differ by
four or sum to four.  Starting from one coordinate and following
the connected support multigraph, all levels must therefore lie in one of
\[
0,4,8,\ldots;\qquad
2,6,10,\ldots;\qquad
1,3,5,\ldots.
\]
The first family would make \(\lVert s\rVert^2\) divisible by \(16\), contrary
to \(200\equiv8\pmod {16}\).

In the second family, a level at least \(10\), together with the other
\(v-1\ge29\) levels, would contribute at least
\(10^2+29\cdot2^2>200\).  Thus only levels \(2\) and \(6\) occur.  If their
multiplicities are \(n_2,n_6\), then
\[
n_2+9n_6=50,\qquad
v=n_2+n_6=50-8n_6\ge30,
\]
so
\[
(n_2,n_6)\in\{(50,0),(41,1),(32,2)\}.
\]
Every root is now either \(e_i+e_j\) with \(s_i=s_j=2\), or
\(e_i-e_j\) with \(s_i=6\) and \(s_j=2\), after ordering the two coordinates.
On a fixed support the sign pattern is unique, and duplicate columns
would have inner product \(2\).  Distinct roots in this family therefore
share at most one coordinate.  At a level-two coordinate, let \(p_i,m_i\)
count positive and negative incidences.  We have \(p_i-m_i=2\).
Each level-six coordinate has six incidences, all paired with negative
incidences at level-two coordinates.  A negative Gram product occurs when
two roots meet at a level-two coordinate with opposite signs.  The shared
coordinate is unique, so each such product is counted once in
\[
P_-=\sum_{i:s_i=2}p_im_i.
\]
Consequently
\[
P_-\equiv\sum_{i:s_i=2} p_im_i
\equiv\sum_{i:s_i=2} m_i
=6n_6
\equiv0\pmod2,
\]
contradicting the parity already proved.

For the odd levels, let \(a_t,c_t\) count coordinates at levels
\(4t+1,4t+3\), respectively.  Sum the signed incidences along each of the
two level chains.  Difference roots cancel within a chain.  The only roots
joining the chains are sum roots \(e_i+e_j\) between levels \(1\) and
\(3\), and they contribute equally to the two sums.  Hence
\[
\sum_{t\ge0}(4t+1)a_t
=\sum_{t\ge0}(4t+3)c_t.
\]
To see the cancellation in the squared levels, write
\[
\begin{aligned}
(4t+1)^2-3&=16t(t+1)-2(4t+1),\\
(4t+3)^2-3&=16t(t+1)+2(4t+3).
\end{aligned}
\]
After multiplying by \(a_t\) and \(c_t\) and summing, the linear terms
cancel by the signed-incidence identity above.  Since
\(\lVert s\rVert^2=200\), this gives
\[
\frac{200-3v}{32}
=\sum_{t\ge1}\binom{t+1}{2}(a_t+c_t).
\]
The right-hand side is an integer.  Hence \(v\equiv24\pmod {32}\), which is impossible for
\(30\le v\le51\).  All three level families are excluded, proving that \(T\)
is disconnected.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact spectra after one or two vertex deletions}
\label{app:small-deletion-spectra}

Let \(M\) be a degree-\(k\) Moore graph of diameter two, and let \(\Delta=\sqrt{4k-3}\).

The matrix in the following theorems is the recomputed distance matrix.
Its correction is already given by Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}:
a surviving nonadjacent pair moves from distance two to distance three
exactly when its unique common neighbour was deleted.

\begin{theorem}[One deleted vertex]\label{thm:one-puncture}
For \(v\in V(M)\), put \(H=M-v\).  Then
\[
 |V(H)|=k^2,
 \qquad \delta^*(H)=k-\frac1k,
 \qquad \lambda_{\min}(D(H))=-2-\sqrt{k}.
\]
The complete distance spectrum is
\begin{align*}
\Spec D(H)={}&\{\rho_+,\rho_-\}
 \cup\{(-2+\sqrt{k})^{(k-1)},(-2-\sqrt{k})^{(k-1)}\}\\
&\cup\left\{\left(-\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right)^{(m_+)},
\left(\frac{\Delta-3}{2}\right)^{(m_-)}\right\},
\end{align*}
where
\[
 \rho_\pm=k^2-2\pm\sqrt{k(k^3-2k^2+3k-1)},
\]
\[
 m_\pm=\frac{k(k-2)(\Delta\pm1)}{2\Delta}.
\]
Thus
\[
 \Phi(H)=k-\frac1k-2-\sqrt{k},
\]
which is positive exactly for integers \(k\ge5\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Endpoints of an edge]\label{thm:edge-puncture}
Let \(uv\in E(M)\) and \(H=M-\{u,v\}\).  For \(k\ge3\),
\[
 |V(H)|=k^2-1,
 \qquad \delta^*(H)=k-\frac2k,
 \qquad \lambda_{\min}(D(H))=-2-\sqrt{k}.
\]
The complete distance spectrum is
\begin{align*}
\Spec D(H)={}&\{\sigma_+,\sigma_-,k-4\}\\
&\cup\{(-2+\sqrt{k})^{(2k-4)},(-2-\sqrt{k})^{(2k-4)}\}\\
&\cup\left\{\left(-\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right)^{(a_+)},
\left(\frac{\Delta-3}{2}\right)^{(a_-)}\right\},
\end{align*}
where
\[
 \sigma_\pm=k^2-3\pm\sqrt{k^4-2k^3+3k^2-8k+7},
\]
\[
 a_+=\frac{(k-2)(k+(k-2)\Delta)}{2\Delta},
 \quad
 a_-=\frac{(k-2)((k-2)\Delta-k)}{2\Delta}.
\]
Thus
\[
 \Phi(H)=k-\frac2k-2-\sqrt{k},
\]
which is positive exactly for integers \(k\ge5\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorems~\ref{thm:one-puncture}
and~\ref{thm:edge-puncture}]
The metric facts stated above give the dual-degree formulas in the cases
\(s=1\) and \(s=2\) of Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}.
We now write the changed-distance matrices in each case and compute their
spectra.

In both cases, we separate the surviving neighbours of the deleted
vertices from the vertices left over.  Vectors constant on each part
give the quotient matrices.  On each neighbour part, choose an orthogonal
basis of the vectors whose entries sum to zero.  For each basis vector,
form a second vector on the remaining vertices: at each vertex, sum the
original entries on its neighbours.  Each pair spans an invariant
two-dimensional block.  The vectors orthogonal to the cell-constant space and all these blocks
give the Moore roots.

For one deleted vertex, put \(A=N(v)\),
\(B=\Gamma_2(v)\), let \(C\) be
the \(A\)-by-\(B\) incidence matrix, and let \(B_0\) be the adjacency
matrix on \(B\).  Only pairs inside \(A\) lose their unique length-two path.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}, their new distance is three.
All other distances are unchanged, so
\[
 D(H)=\begin{pmatrix}3(J-I)&2J-C\\2J-C^{\mathsf T}&2(J-I)-B_0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
The normalized constant quotient is
\[
 Q_1=\begin{pmatrix}
 3(k-1)&(2k-1)\sqrt{k-1}\\
 (2k-1)\sqrt{k-1}&2k^2-3k-1
 \end{pmatrix},
\]
whose eigenvalues are \(\rho_\pm\).  Block comparison in the Moore identity
gives
\[
 CC^{\mathsf T}=(k-1)I,\qquad
 CB_0=J-C,\qquad
 C^{\mathsf T}\one_A=\one_B,
\]
\[
 B_0^2+C^{\mathsf T}C=(k-1)I-B_0+J.
\]
Thus \(C^{\mathsf T}\) is injective and
\[
 \mathbb R^B=
 \langle\one_B\rangle\perp
 C^{\mathsf T}(\one_A^\perp)\perp K,
 \qquad K=\ker C,\qquad \dim K=k(k-2).
\]
Choose an orthogonal basis \(\mathcal B\) of \(\one_A^\perp\), and for
\(z\in\mathcal B\) put
\[
 W_z=\operatorname{span}\{(z,0),(0,C^{\mathsf T}z)\}.
\]
The quotient acts on the cell-constant space \(W_{\mathrm{const}}\).
The remaining directions split into the two-dimensional spaces \(W_z\)
for \(z\in\mathcal B\) and the residual space \(0\oplus K\).
The identities above show that these spaces are invariant and mutually
orthogonal.  Put
\[
 W_{\mathrm{inc}}=\bigoplus_{z\in\mathcal B}W_z.
\]
Consequently
\[
\mathbb R^{V(H)}=W_{\mathrm{const}}\perp W_{\mathrm{inc}}\perp(0\oplus K),
 \qquad 2+2(k-1)+k(k-2)=k^2.
\]
On a zero-sum incidence space, the action in the chosen generators is
\(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-3&-(k-1)\\-1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\),
with eigenvalues \(-2\pm\sqrt{k}\).  The restriction to \(K\) satisfies
\(B_0^2+B_0-(k-1)I=0\) and \(D=-2I-B_0\), so only the two Moore roots
remain.  Their multiplicities are determined by the trace of \(B_0\):
after removing its principal eigenvalue \(k-1\) and its \(k-1\) copies
of \(-1\) on \(C^{\mathsf T}(\one_A^\perp)\), the remaining trace is
zero.  This trace and the residual dimension give \(m_\pm\).

For an adjacent deleted pair, put
\[
 A=N(u)\setminus\{v\},\qquad B=N(v)\setminus\{u\},\qquad
 C=V(M)\setminus\bigl(\{u,v\}\cup A\cup B\bigr).
\]
Only pairs inside \(A\) or inside \(B\) lose a length-two path.  Since \(k\ge3\), Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture} applies to the
two deleted vertices and makes the new distance of each such pair three.  The antisymmetric constant line has eigenvalue
\(k-4\), and the normalized symmetric quotient is
\[
 Q_2=\begin{pmatrix}
 5k-8&\sqrt{(k-1)(2k-3)(4k-6)}\\
 \sqrt{(k-1)(2k-3)(4k-6)}&2k^2-5k+2
 \end{pmatrix},
\]
with eigenvalues \(\sigma_\pm\).  Let \(R_A,R_B\) be the incidence matrices
from \(A,B\) to \(C\), and let \(T\) be the adjacency matrix on \(C\).  The
Moore identity gives
\[
 R_AR_A^{\mathsf T}=R_BR_B^{\mathsf T}=(k-1)I,\qquad
 R_AR_B^{\mathsf T}=J,
\]
\[
 R_AT=J-R_A,\qquad R_BT=J-R_B,
\]
\[
 R_A^{\mathsf T}R_A+R_B^{\mathsf T}R_B+T^2
 =(k-1)I-T+J.
\]
The transpose maps of \(R_A\) and \(R_B\), restricted to their cell
zero-sum spaces, are injective and have orthogonal images.  Pair each zero-sum direction in its
original cell with its incidence image.
The resulting invariant two-dimensional spaces carry
\(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-3&-(k-1)\\-1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\),
as in the one-vertex calculation.  The residual space
\[
 K=\ker R_A\cap\ker R_B,\qquad \dim K=(k-2)^2,
\]
is invariant and satisfies
\[
 T^2+T-(k-1)I=0,\qquad D=-2I-T.
\]
These spaces and the three-dimensional cell-constant space are mutually
orthogonal and complete, since
\[
 3+2(2k-4)+(k-2)^2=k^2-1.
\]
The constant direction of \(T\) has eigenvalue \(k-2\), while its two
zero-sum incidence images contribute trace \(-2(k-2)\).  Since
\(\tr T=0\), one has \(\tr(T|_K)=k-2\), and dimension and trace give
\(a_\pm\).  The residual negative root is greater than
\(-2-\sqrt{k}\).  For \(k\ge3\), the antisymmetric constant root also satisfies
\[
 (k-4)-(-2-\sqrt{k})=k-2+\sqrt{k}>0.
\]
It remains to compare the incidence root with the smaller
constant-quotient roots.  We shift the quotients by the negative of the
candidate root and test positive definiteness.  In each case the leading
diagonal entry is positive, and
\[
 \det(Q_1+(2+\sqrt{k})I)
 =k(2k^2+2k^{3/2}-3k+2)>0,
\]
\[
 \det(Q_2+(2+\sqrt{k})I)
 =(\sqrt{k}-1)(\sqrt{k}+1)
 (2k^2+2k^{3/2}-3k+2\sqrt{k}+6)>0.
\]
The shifted symmetric quotients are therefore positive definite.  The
constant-quotient eigenvalues lie strictly above the incidence root,
completing the comparison.
Exact quotient normalization, trace-to-multiplicity equations, replacement
paths, and all least-root comparisons are independently checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_05_small_moore_punctures.py}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Two nonadjacent deleted vertices]\label{thm:nonadjacent-puncture}
Let \(u,v\) be nonadjacent vertices of \(M\), \(k\ge5\), and put
\(H=M-\{u,v\}\).  Define
\begin{align*}
R_k(x)={}&x^4+(10-2k^2)x^3+(2k^3-17k^2-2k+36)x^2\\
&+(12k^3-49k^2-4k+53)x\\
&-2k^4+17k^3-38k^2+5k+20,
\end{align*}
\[
 M_- =\frac{k(k-2)+(k^2-4k+2)\Delta}{2\Delta},
 \quad
 M_+ =\frac{-k(k-2)+(k^2-4k+2)\Delta}{2\Delta}.
\]
Then
\begin{align*}
\chi_{D(H)}(x)={}&(x-k+3)R_k(x)
 (x^2+4x-k+3)^{k-2}\\
&\cdot(x^2+4x-k+5)^{k-2}
 \left(x+\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right)^{M_-}\\
&\cdot\left(x-\frac{\Delta-3}{2}\right)^{M_+}.
\end{align*}
Moreover,
\[
 \delta^*(H)=k-\frac2k,
\]
and \(H\) is a strict counterexample for every realizable integer \(k\ge6\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The deleted vertices have a unique common neighbour \(w\).  With
\(A=N(u)\setminus\{w\}\), \(B=N(v)\setminus\{w\}\),
\(C=N(w)\setminus\{u,v\}\), and
\[
 Z=V(M)\setminus\bigl(\{u,v,w\}\cup A\cup B\cup C\bigr),
\]
the surviving graph has cell sizes, in the order \(\{w\},A,B,C,Z\),
\[
 1,\quad k-1,\quad k-1,\quad k-2,\quad (k-1)(k-2).
\]
The Moore common-neighbour rule gives a perfect matching between
\(A\) and \(B\), and the five-cell partition is equitable.
The distance rule from Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}, stated at the
start of this section, applies because \(2\le k-1\).
If the unique length-two path between two surviving vertices passed
through \(u\) or \(v\), a replacement path survives and their new distance
is exactly three.
We now restrict the distance matrix to vectors constant on each cell.
The row-sum quotient is
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
0&3k-3&3k-3&k-2&2(k-1)(k-2)\\
3&3k-6&2k-3&2k-4&2k^2-7k+6\\
3&2k-3&3k-6&2k-4&2k^2-7k+6\\
1&2k-2&2k-2&2k-6&2k^2-7k+5\\
2&2k-3&2k-3&2k-5&2k^2-7k+5
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its characteristic polynomial is \((x-k+3)R_k(x)\).

Identify \(\mathbb R^A\) with \(\mathbb R^B\) through the perfect matching,
and let \(R_A,R_B,R_C\) be the incidence matrices from the three
nonconstant cells to \(Z\).  Let \(T\) be the adjacency matrix on \(Z\).
The Moore identity gives
\[
\begin{gathered}
R_AR_A^{\mathsf T}=R_BR_B^{\mathsf T}=(k-2)I,\quad
R_CR_C^{\mathsf T}=(k-1)I,\\
R_AR_B^{\mathsf T}=J-I,\quad
R_AR_C^{\mathsf T}=R_BR_C^{\mathsf T}=J,\\
R_AT+R_B=J-R_A,\quad
R_BT+R_A=J-R_B,\quad
R_CT=J-R_C,\\
R_A^{\mathsf T}R_A+R_B^{\mathsf T}R_B+R_C^{\mathsf T}R_C+T^2
=(k-1)I-T+J.
\end{gathered}
\]
Moreover, every vertex of \(Z\) has one neighbour in each of \(A,B,C\),
so
\[
 R_A^{\mathsf T}\one_A
 =R_B^{\mathsf T}\one_B
 =R_C^{\mathsf T}\one_C
 =\one_Z,
 \qquad T\one_Z=(k-3)\one_Z.
\]

Use the coordinate order \((\{w\},A,B,C,Z)\).  Choose orthogonal bases
\(\mathcal B_A\) of \(\one_A^\perp\) and
\(\mathcal B_C\) of \(\one_C^\perp\).  For
\(\xi\in\mathcal B_A\) and \(\eta\in\mathcal B_C\), put
\[
\begin{aligned}
u_\xi^\pm&=(0,\xi,\pm\xi,0,0),&
v_\xi^\pm&=(0,0,0,0,
 (R_A^{\mathsf T}\pm R_B^{\mathsf T})\xi),\\
u_\eta^C&=(0,0,0,\eta,0),&
v_\eta^C&=(0,0,0,0,R_C^{\mathsf T}\eta),
\end{aligned}
\]
and define
\[
 W_\xi^\pm=\operatorname{span}\{u_\xi^\pm,v_\xi^\pm\},
 \qquad
 W_\eta^C=\operatorname{span}\{u_\eta^C,v_\eta^C\}.
\]
The incidence identities ensure that these generators give genuine
two-dimensional spaces: the relevant maps are injective.  They also show
that the spaces are mutually orthogonal and orthogonal to the
five-dimensional cell-constant space \(W_{\mathrm{const}}\).
For the residual space
\[
 K=\ker R_A\cap\ker R_B\cap\ker R_C
\]
one has
\[
 K\subseteq\one_Z^\perp,\qquad
 \dim K=(k-2)(k-4),\qquad T(K)\subseteq K.
\]
Thus \(W_{\mathrm{const}}\), the modules \(W_\xi^\pm,W_\eta^C\), and
\(0\oplus0\oplus0\oplus0\oplus K\) form an orthogonal direct sum, since
\[
 5+4(k-2)+2(k-3)+(k-2)(k-4)=k^2-1.
\]

In the ordered generator pairs
\((u_\xi^+,v_\xi^+)\), \((u_\xi^-,v_\xi^-)\), and
\((u_\eta^C,v_\eta^C)\), respectively, the distance operator has matrices
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}-4&-(k-3)\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \begin{pmatrix}-2&-(k-1)\\-1&-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \begin{pmatrix}-2&-(k-1)\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
These give the two displayed quadratic factors and \(k-3\) copies of
each Moore linear factor.  On \(K\), the \(Z\)-block identity gives
\[
 T^2+T-(k-1)I=0,
 \qquad D(H)|_K=-2I-T.
\]
The constant direction of \(Z\) has \(T\)-eigenvalue \(k-3\), while the
\(Z\)-images in the symmetric, antisymmetric, and common-neighbour
modules have eigenvalues \(-2,0,-1\), with dimensions
\(k-2,k-2,k-3\), respectively.  Since \(\tr T=0\),
\[
 \tr(T|_K)=2(k-2).
\]
The residual adjacency operator has just two possible eigenvalues, so its
dimension and trace determine both multiplicities.  We add the Moore-root
copies from the common-neighbour spaces to obtain the full exponents
\(M_-\) and \(M_+\).

This completes the spectral factorization.  The value
\(\delta^*(H)=k-\frac2k\) again follows from the \(s=2\) case of
Theorem~\ref{thm:small-puncture}.  To prove strictness, it is simpler to
bound the distance correction than to locate the least root of the
quartic.  Put
\[
 E_S=D(H)-D(M)[V(H)].
\]
The correction \(E_S\) records exactly the pairs whose distance has
increased.  Up to a simultaneous permutation of rows and columns, it is
the adjacency matrix of two copies of \(K_k\) meeting in \(w\), together
with \(k(k-2)\) isolated vertices.  Therefore
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(E_S)=\frac{k-2-\sqrt{k^2+4k-4}}2.
\]
Since the parent Moore graph has score
\(k-(3+\Delta)/2\) and deletion lowers the minimum dual degree by
\(2/k\),
Proposition~\ref{prop:deletion-stability} gives
\[
 \Phi(H)\ge
 \frac{3k-5-\Delta-\sqrt{k^2+4k-4}}2-\frac2k.
\]
For \(k\ge7\), one has
\[
 \sqrt{4k-3}<k-1,
 \qquad
 \sqrt{k^2+4k-4}<k+2.
\]
Indeed, the corresponding differences of squares are
\(k(k-6)+4>0\) and \(8>0\).  The preceding lower bound is therefore
greater than
\[
 \frac{k-6}{2}-\frac2k
 \ge\frac12-\frac27=\frac3{14}>0.
\]
For \(k=6\), use \(\sqrt{21}<23/5\) and \(\sqrt{56}<15/2\), which
follow from \(529>525\) and \(225>224\).  The same lower bound is then
greater than
\[
 \frac{13-23/5-15/2}{2}-\frac13=\frac7{60}>0.
\]
Thus \(\Phi(H)>0\) for every realizable integer \(k\ge6\).
The direct-sum decomposition, injectivity, orthogonality, all recomputed
distances, and the strict comparison
are independently checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_03_nonadjacent_puncture.py} and
\codefile{scripts/verify_research_extensions_exact.py}.
\end{proof}


\section{The distance spectrum of a Moore second sphere}
\label{app:second-sphere-proof}

This is the complete proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:second-subconstituent}.
The main-text statement records the graph parameters and the resulting
distance eigenvalue; here we give the incidence blocks and their
multiplicities.

\begin{proof}
Each vertex of the second sphere loses exactly one neighbour when we
pass to its induced graph.  Put \(m=K(K-1)\); the Moore property gives
\(\lvert\Gamma_2(v)\rvert=m\).  Each vertex of \(\Gamma_2(v)\) has its
single parent in \(N(v)\), while each vertex of \(N(v)\) has \(K-1\)
neighbours in \(\Gamma_2(v)\).  The induced graph \(X\) consequently has
order \(m\) and degree \((K-1)\).  It contains no triangle or four-cycle,
since \(M\) contains neither.  To obtain the distance spectrum we now
calculate the adjacency spectrum and then prove that the diameter is three.

Relative to \(\{v\}\sqcup N(v)\sqcup\Gamma_2(v)\), let
\(C\in\{0,1\}^{K\times m}\) be the incidence block from \(N(v)\) to
\(\Gamma_2(v)\), and let \(B\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}\) be the adjacency
matrix of \(X\).  Write \(J_{a,b}\) for the \(a\times b\) all-ones matrix
and \(J_a=J_{a,a}\).  Then
\[
 C\one_m=(K-1)\one_K,
 \qquad
 C^{\mathsf T}\one_K=\one_m.
\]
The Moore identity \cite[Section~4]{HoffmanSingleton1960}, read in the
corresponding blocks, gives
\[
 CC^{\mathsf T}=(K-1)I_K,
 \qquad
 CB=J_{K,m}-C,
\]
and
\[
 C^{\mathsf T}C+B^2=(K-1)I_m-B+J_m.
\]
Since \(CC^{\mathsf T}=(K-1)I_K\), the map \(C^{\mathsf T}\) is injective.
The incidence block therefore gives the orthogonal decomposition
\[
 \mathbb R^m
 =\langle\one_m\rangle\perp
 C^{\mathsf T}(\one_K^\perp)\perp\ker C.
\]
The first space is the principal adjacency eigenspace, because
\(B\one_m=(K-1)\one_m\).  Transposing the identity for \(CB\)
shows that \(B\) acts as \(-I\) on
\(C^{\mathsf T}(\one_K^\perp)\).  The same identity makes
\(\ker C\) invariant under \(B\); moreover,
\(\ker C\subseteq\one_m^\perp\).  The final block identity restricts there to
\[
 (B^2+B-(K-1)I_m)|_{\ker C}=0.
\]
Thus the remaining eigenvalues are among
\[
 r,s=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{4K-3}}2.
\]
Finally,
\[
 \dim\ker C=m-K=K(K-2),
 \qquad
 \tr(B|_{\ker C})=0,
\]
because the eigenvalue \(K-1\) cancels the \(K-1\) copies of \(-1\)
in \(\tr B=0\).  Hence the multiplicities \(p,q\) of \(r,s\) satisfy
\[
 p+q=K(K-2),\qquad pr+qs=0,
\]
so
\[
 p=\frac{K(K-2)(1+\sqrt{4K-3})}{2\sqrt{4K-3}},
 \qquad
 q=\frac{K(K-2)(\sqrt{4K-3}-1)}{2\sqrt{4K-3}}.
\]
Both roots occur.  It remains to check that \(X\) has diameter three.
If the unique common neighbour in \(M\) of nonadjacent vertices \(x,y\in X\)
lies in \(X\), their distance in \(X\) is two.  The only other
possibility is their common parent in \(N(v)\).  Choose
\(b\in N_X(x)\), and let \(p\) be the common parent of \(x,y\).
The parent of \(b\) is not \(p\), since otherwise \(p,x,b\) would form
a triangle.
Moreover \(b\not\sim y\), and the unique common neighbour
\(c\) of \(b,y\) belongs to \(X\): it cannot be \(v\), and it cannot
lie in \(N(v)\), since \(b\) and \(y\) have different parents there.
Thus \(x-b-c-y\) is a path in \(X\).  Each parent has \(K-1\ge2\)
children, and pairs of distinct children have no length-two path in \(X\).
Thus \(X\) has diameter three and
Theorem~\ref{thm:diameter-three-score} applies.  Among the nonprincipal
adjacency eigenvalues, \((-1+\sqrt{4K-3})/2\) maximizes
\(|\theta+1|\); substitution gives
\[
 \lambda_{\min}(D(X))=-\frac{5+\sqrt{4K-3}}2.
\]
For \(K=3,4,5\), direct comparison gives a nonpositive score.  For
\(K\ge6\), both sides of the relevant comparison are positive, and the
threshold reduces to
\[
 (2K-7)^2-(4K-3)=4(K^2-8K+13)>0
\]
because \(K>4+\sqrt3\).  The finite \(K=7\) instance is checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_wow284_38_40_42.py}.
\end{proof}

\section{Positivity of the dual LP slack}
\label{app:lp-slack-positivity}

Use the measure, support points, and slack notation from the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:lp-ceiling}.  We verify the positivity of every slack beyond
degree four, as used in its optimality and rigidity argument.

We check the first five values exactly and then
bound the tail.  For \(5\le i\le9\), removing the common positive factor
\(k(k-1)(k+2)(k^2+3)/6\) leaves, respectively,
\[
 2,\quad 5k-13,\quad 2(3k^2-17k+25),
\]
\[
 6k^3-47k^2+139k-150,
 \quad
 2(3k^4-27k^3+106k^2-219k+194),
\]
all positive for \(k\ge4\); for the nontrivial residual factors this follows
after writing \(k=m+4\), when all coefficients are nonnegative and the
constant terms are positive.  For \(i\ge10\), put \(r=k-1\ge3\).
The support lies in \([-2\sqrt r,2\sqrt r]\), because
\(1+\sqrt{2r}\le2\sqrt r\).  For \(|z|\le1\), the recurrence gives
\[
 F_i(2\sqrt r\,z)
 =r^{i/2}U_i(z)-r^{(i-2)/2}U_{i-2}(z),
\]
where \(U_j\) is the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind.
Using \(|U_j(z)|\le j+1\), the total mass \(B_k-1\), and
\(F_i(k)=kr^{i-1}\), we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{|\mu(F_i)|}{F_i(k)}
 &\le \frac{r^2+4r+6}{6}\,r^{-i/2}\bigl((i+1)r+i-1\bigr)\\
 &\le \frac{2i+1}{3}\,r^{3-i/2}
 \le \frac{2i+1}{3}\,3^{3-i/2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last two inequalities use \(r\ge3\) and \(i\ge10\).
At \(i=10\) the right-hand side is \(7/9\), and it decreases thereafter
because \(3(2i+1)^2-(2i+3)^2=8i^2-6>0\).  Hence
\[
 \frac{|\mu(F_i)|}{F_i(k)}<1\qquad(i\ge10).
\]
The measure's contribution has smaller absolute value than the principal
contribution.  Their sum, and therefore every slack in the tail, is positive.

\section{Spectral exclusions for two construction families}
\label{app:excluded-constructions}

The prime-field family below does not produce strict counterexamples of
diameter three. None of the layer-respecting perfect-matching deletions
considered in the second subsection is a strict counterexample.  Their
definitions and proofs are kept together so the coordinate calculations can
be read independently of the principal counterexamples and deletion bounds.

\subsection{The prime-field construction in diameter three}

For an odd prime \(q\ge7\) and \(1\le m\le q\), define \(G(q,m)\) on
vertices \(P_{i,j},Q_{k,\ell}\), with \(0\le i,k<m\) and
\(j,\ell\in\mathbb F_q\), by
\begin{align*}
P_{i,j}&\sim P_{i,j\pm1},\\
Q_{k,\ell}&\sim Q_{k,\ell\pm2},\\
P_{i,j}&\sim Q_{k,ik+j}.
\end{align*}
This is a balanced specialization of known finite-field girth-five
constructions \cite{AbreuEtAl2008}.  It is \((m+2)\)-regular.  A coordinate
common-neighbour calculation shows that it has no triangle or \(4\)-cycle for
\(q\ge7\): for a cross pair, the possible same-side common neighbours require
residues in the disjoint sets \(\{\pm1\}\) and \(\{\pm2\}\).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:prime-field}
If \(G(q,m)\) has diameter three, then it is not a strict counterexample to
WOW-284.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The degree restriction first excludes \(m\le3\) by
Theorem~\ref{thm:regular-degree-six}.  For the remaining degrees, we first
consider vectors that assign the same value to all vertices of a given
type with a fixed first coordinate.  Taking neighbour sums leaves these
vectors constant on the same sets.  The restricted adjacency matrix has
eigenvalues \(m+2\), \(2-m\), and \(2\) with multiplicity \(2m-2\);
the shifted WOW window leaves only \(m\in\{4,5,6\}\).

This restriction is the zero-frequency block of the Fourier transform
in the second coordinate.  We now use a nonzero character to exclude
the last three cases.
Let \(\omega=e^{2\pi\mathrm i/q}\).  On the nonzero
character \(t=1\), the adjacency block has form
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}aI&M\\M^*&bI\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad a=2\cos\frac{2\pi}{q},
 \quad b=2\cos\frac{4\pi}{q},
 \quad M_{ik}=\omega^{ik}.
\]
If \(\sigma\) is a singular value of \(M\), the associated two-dimensional
invariant subspace carries
\(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&\sigma\\\sigma&b\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\).
We need only the largest singular value, rather than the full Fourier
spectrum.  Since \(\|M\|_F^2=m^2\), one singular value satisfies
\(\sigma^2\ge m\).  Its two-dimensional block therefore supplies a
nonprincipal eigenvalue at least
\[
 \sqrt m+\frac{a+b}{2}
 \ge \sqrt m+\cos(\pi/7)-\frac12.
\]
For \(q\ge7\), both \(\cos(2\pi/q)\) and \(\cos(4\pi/q)\) increase with
\(q\), so their sum is minimized at \(q=7\).  The last inequality then uses
\(2\cos(2\pi/7)+2\cos(4\pi/7)=2\cos(\pi/7)-1\).
Moreover \(\cos(\pi/7)>\sqrt3/2\), and
\(h(m)=\sqrt{2m+2}-\sqrt m\) is increasing for \(m\ge1\) and satisfies
\[
 h(m)\le h(6)=\sqrt{14}-\sqrt6
 <\frac{27}{20}<\frac{\sqrt3+1}{2}.
\]
Thus the displayed nonprincipal eigenvalue lies above
\(-1+\sqrt{2m+2}\) for \(m=4,5,6\).  The common-neighbour
case split, Fourier reduction, radical comparisons, and exact \(q=7\) controls
are independently checked by
\codefile{scripts/verify_proof_audit_08_prime_field.py}.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact certificates and the scope of Lean verification}\label{sec:verification}

The computer-assisted arguments have three specific tasks: certify the
labelled finite graphs, exhaust the stated finite orbit families, and check
symbolic identities derived in the text.  No theorem-level sign or
eigenvalue ordering uses floating-point arithmetic.  The computations use
SymPy and NetworkX \cite{MeurerEtAl2017,HagbergSchultSwart2008}.
The Lean results below provide a separate layer of formal verification.

The historical release \texttt{\RepoTag} archives the labelled graph data,
exact rational and polynomial certificates, and finite-orbit records used
for the computer-assisted steps.  The analytic additions to this v2 are
proved in the text.

For the explicit \(50\)-vertex graph, Lean 4.31 with Mathlib 4.31
\cite{deMouraUllrich2021,Mathlib2020} checks structural and exact rational
matrix certificates.  The development pins the toolchain and dependency
revision in \codefile{lean/lean-toolchain} and
\codefile{lean/lake-manifest.json}.  It checks the coordinate graph,
regularity, the exhaustive common-neighbour certificate, girth five,
the adjacency-square identity, the algebraic formula for the case-defined
\(0/1/2\) matrix, and an exact rational diagonalization with multiplicities.
The diameter-two certificate identifies that matrix with the actual
distance matrix in the mathematical proof.  Regularity and diagonalization
then give
\[
 \delta^*(G)=7,\qquad \lambda_{\min}(D(G))=-4,\qquad \Phi(G)=3.
\]
In the historical certificate baseline these conclusions are assembled in
the paper rather than exported as a combined graph-distance theorem.

Lean also kernel-checks finite spectral certificates attached to the explicit
constructions of orders \(38,39,40,42\).  At orders \(38,39,42\), they
certify the dual-degree data and positive definiteness of the relevant shifted
distance matrix.  At order \(40\), they certify an invertible exact
diagonalization, multiplicities, least eigenvalue \(-5\), dual degree six,
and gap one.  These are finite matrix certificates rather than end-to-end
\texttt{SimpleGraph.dist} formalizations.

Separately, Lean formalizes the analytic optimization statement in
Theorem~\ref{thm:lp-ceiling}.  For every integer \(k\ge4\) and every admissible
finitely supported expansion
\[
  f=\sum_i c_iF_i,
  \qquad c_0>0,
  \qquad c_i\ge0\quad(i\ge5),
  \qquad f\vert_{I_k}\le0,
\]
it proves
\[
  B_kc_0\le f(k),
  \qquad B_k=\frac{(k+2)(k^2+3)}6.
\]
The formal development defines the coefficient family of the displayed
quartic \(f_*\), proves that it is admissible and attains equality, and proves
that the equality cases are exactly the positive scalar multiples of \(f_*\),
both as polynomials and at coefficient level.  The public Lean development is
sorry-free and kernel-checked by Lean 4.31.

This LP formalization is deliberately graph-independent.  It does not
formalize the trace interpretation of the \(F_i(A)\), the girth-five
vanishing and nonnegativity statements, or the passage from the LP inequality
to graph-order bounds.  The integral-slack consequences, signed-complement
theory, puncture spectra, and deletion results lie outside this Lean
development.  They are proved in the text using the analytic and, where
explicitly identified, exact computer-assisted components.  This is the
precise scope of the Lean claims in the paper.
